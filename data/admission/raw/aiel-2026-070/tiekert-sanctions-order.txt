II.    LEGAL STANDARD

       Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11 requires that “[e]very pleading, written motion, and

other paper must be signed by at least one attorney of record in the attorney’s name.” Fed. R.

Civ. P. 11(a). Rule 11 further states, in relevant part:

       By presenting to the court a pleading, written motion, or other paper – whether by
       signing, filing, submitting, or later advocating it – an attorney . . . certifies that to
       the best of the person’s knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an
       inquiry reasonable under the circumstances . . . the claims, defenses, and other
       legal contentions are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for
       extending, modifying, or reversing existing law or for establishing new law[.]

Id. 11(b)(2). “When a party’s legal contentions are challenged as violating Rule 11, ‘the

operative question is whether the argument is frivolous, i.e., the legal position has no chance of

success, and there is no reasonable argument to extend, modify, or reverse the law as it stands.’”

Black v. Ganieva, No. 21-CV-8824, 2022 WL 2354916, at *13 (S.D.N.Y. June 30, 2022)

(quoting Fishoff v. Coty Inc., 634 F.3d 647, 654 (2d Cir. 2011)).2 “A legal argument may be

sanctioned as frivolous when it amounts to an abuse of the adversary system,” and “[t]he filing

of papers without taking the necessary care in their preparation is an abuse of the judicial system

that is subject to Rule 11 sanction.” Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443, 460 (S.D.N.Y.

2023). “In short, Rule 11(b) imposes an affirmative duty on parties and counsel to ensure that all

papers filed with the Court are factually and legally well-founded.” Barteca Holdings LLC v.

Tacobarn Newtown LLC, No. 26-CV-250, 2026 WL 2253785, at *2 (D. Conn. Aug. 4, 2026).

“At the very least, the duties imposed by Rule 11 require that attorneys read, and thereby confirm

the existence and validity of, the legal authorities on which they rely.” Park, 91 F.4th at 615.

       Where another party is moving for sanctions under Rule 11, the party must do so “in a

filing that is ‘separate[ ] from any other motion’ and that ‘describe[s] the specific conduct that

allegedly violates Rule 11(b).’” Kyros L. P.C. v. World Wrestling Ent., Inc., 78 F.4th 532, 543

(2d Cir. 2023) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(2)). The party may not present the motion to the

court until after the passage of a twenty-one-day safe harbor period, during which the person

against whom the motion is brought can withdraw or correct the challenged filing. See id.

Additionally, “[o]n its own, the court may order an attorney . . . to show cause why conduct

specifically described in the order has not violated Rule 11(b).” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(3); see

Kyros L., 78 F.4th at 543.

       When Rule 11 has been violated, district courts have broad discretion to craft a sanction

appropriate under the circumstances. See Star Mark Mgmt., Inc. v. Koon Chun Hing Kee Soy &

Sauce Factory, Ltd., 682 F.3d 170, 179 (2d Cir. 2012) (per curiam). Any sanctions decision,

however, should be “made with restraint” because “the trial court may act as accuser, fact finder

and sentencing judge.” Storey v. Cello Holdings, L.L.C., 347 F.3d 370, 387 (2d Cir. 2003).

“When a court initiates Rule 11 sanctions sua sponte and the opportunity to correct or withdraw

the challenged submission is unavailable, the court must make a finding of bad faith on the part

of the attorney before imposing the sanctions.” Kyros L., 78 F.4th at 543. The Second Circuit

has reasoned that “sua sponte [sanctions] without affording the offender the opportunity to

withdraw the challenged document in the manner provided in the ‘safe harbor’ provision of Rule

11(c)(1)(A), is akin to the court’s inherent power of contempt,” which requires a finding of

subjective bad faith. Muhammad v. Walmart Stores E., L.P., 732 F.3d 104, 108 (2d Cir. 2013)

(per curiam).

III.   DISCUSSION

               Rule 11 Violation

       Amid the growing use of GenAI in legal research and writing, district courts are

increasingly faced with the concerning problem of hallucinated legal authority. See, e.g.,

Barteca Holdings, 2026 WL 2253785, at *1; Jimenez-Fogarty v. Fogarty, 831 F. Supp. 3d 253,

257-59 (S.D.N.Y. 2026); Kaur v. Desso, No. 25-CV-726, 2025 WL 1895859, at *1 (N.D.N.Y.

July 9, 2025). As one district court described the phenomenon, AI-generated hallucinations

often take one of three forms: (1) fabricated cases, meaning a citation to a decision that simply

does not exist or is a real decision but is wholly unrelated; (2) fabricated quotations from an

actual case; and (3) misstatements of law, including misrepresentations of case holdings. See

Andre v. Warden, FCI Danbury, 827 F. Supp. 3d 294, 305 (D. Conn. 2025). “There is no

question” that all three types of errors violate Rule 11(b) because reliance on any of them “does

not constitute ‘existing law’ and certainly does not provide to the Court a nonfrivolous argument

for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law or for establishing new law.” Lexos Media

IP, LLC v. Overstock.com, Inc., No. 22-2324, 2026 WL 265581, at *10 (D. Kan. Feb. 2, 2026);

see Barteca Holdings, 2026 WL 2253785, at *6-7 (finding Rule 11 violation where attorney

relied on AI-hallucinated quotes and erroneous legal propositions but not wholly fabricated

cases). Rule 11 is not violated simply because attorneys use GenAI, but rather because their

inclusion of GenAI hallucinations in a filing demonstrates that they did not “read, and thereby

confirm the existence and validity of, the legal authorities on which they rely.” Park, 91 F.4th at

615.

       Mr. Higbie violated Rule 11, which he readily admits. For each citation that the Court

raised during the Bench Ruling, Mr. Higbie stated he either relied on a nonexistent quotation or

misrepresented the holding or a legal statement in the decision. (See Higbie Resp. at 2-6.) Mr.

Higbie’s “verification” was also wholly deficient to the point of being non-existent. Rather than

matter. (Id. at 2; see id. at 3 (“I did not verify the veracity of the characterizations of the cases

See Barteca Holdings, 2026 WL 2253785, at *4; Kaur, 2025 WL 1895859, at *3; Benjamin v.

Costco Wholesale Corp., 779 F. Supp. 3d 341, 348 (E.D.N.Y. 2025).

                Bad Faith

        “Generally, subjective bad faith requires a showing that the attorney has actual

knowledge that a pleading or argument that he or she is advancing is frivolous.” Kaur, 2025 WL

1895859, at *3 (emphasis omitted). But “[a]n assertion may be made in subjective bad faith

even when it was based in confusion.” Mata, 678 F. Supp. 3d at 463. Additionally, “[t]he

requisite actual knowledge may be demonstrated by circumstantial evidence and inferred from

conscious avoidance.” Estevez v. Berkeley Coll., No. 18-CV-10350, 2022 WL 17177971, at *1

(S.D.N.Y. Nov. 23, 2022). “The conscious avoidance test is met when a person consciously

avoided learning a fact while aware of a high probability of its existence, unless the factfinder is

persuaded that the person actually believed the contrary.” Mata, 678 F. Supp. 3d at 463.

Applying this standard, courts within the Second Circuit have found that reliance on nonexistent

legal authority fabricated by GenAI constitutes bad faith under Rule 11. See, e.g., Barteca

Holdings, 2026 WL 2253785, at *4; Jimenez-Fogarty, 831 F. Supp. 3d at 264; Benjamin, 779 F.

Supp. 3d at 350. In Jimenez-Fogarty, the court identified two cases in which the presentation of

       Finally, I consider Defendants’ counsel’s requests for attorney’s fees. The text of Rule

11 and Second Circuit precedent are clear on this issue. A sanction imposed under Rule 11 may

include an order directing payment of reasonable attorney’s fees “if imposed on motion.” Fed.

R. Civ. P. 11(c)(4). “By its terms, the rule thus precludes a court from awarding attorneys’ fees

on its own initiative.” Nuwesra, 174 F.3d at 94. “[A]bsent a specific motion for attorneys’ fees,

[a district] court only ha[s] authority to order sanctions payable to the court.” Baffa v.

Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette Sec. Corp., 222 F.3d 52, 57 (2d Cir. 2000); accord Dakus v.

Koninklijke Luchtvaart Maatschappij, N.V., No. 22-CV-7962, 2024 WL 4265646, at *6

(S.D.N.Y. Sep. 23, 2024); Castro v. Mitchell, 727 F. Supp. 2d 302, 309-10 (S.D.N.Y. 2010).

Defendants did not file a separate Rule 11 motion, and Murphy’s argument in his Reply seeking

dismissal under Rule 41(b) for failure to comply with Rule 11, (see Murphy Reply at 8), is not a

motion. I noted during the Bench Ruling that neither Defendant had made a Rule 11 motion, and

cited Rule 11(c)(3) in ordering Mr. Higbie to show cause. (See Tr. 32:17-33:3.) Defendants’

requests are therefore denied. While the Court appreciates that Defendants’ counsel brought the

issue of Mr. Higbie’s citations to its attention, they are also reminded of the need to closely read

the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and related precedent before making such a request.
