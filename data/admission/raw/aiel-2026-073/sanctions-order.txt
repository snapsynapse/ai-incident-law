                                           DISCUSSION

   I.      Legal Standard

        A district court may impose sanctions under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11 when a

lawyer “submits frivolous legal arguments—those not warranted by existing law or by a

nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law or for establishing

new law.” Bell v. Vacuforce, LLC, 908 F.3d 1075, 1079 (7th Cir. 2018) (quotation marks

omitted); Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b). “If, after notice and a reasonable opportunity to respond, the

court determines that Rule 11(b) has been violated, the court may impose an appropriate sanction

on any attorney . . . that violated the rule or is responsible for the violation.” Fed. R. Civ. P.

11(c)(1). “[S]anctions are a collateral matter, which courts generally can resolve after a

judgment is rendered.” Hurston v. Ind. Gaming Co. LLC, No. 23-1099, 2023 WL 8111460, at *3

(7th Cir. Nov. 22, 2023). The appropriateness of imposing Rule 11 sanctions requires “an



                                                    2
             4:25-cv-04217-SLD-RLH           # 33       Filed: 09/02/26     Page 3 of 11




objective determination of whether a sanctioned party’s conduct was reasonable under the

circumstances.” Brown v. Fed’n of State Med. Bds. of the U.S., 830 F.2d 1429, 1435 (7th Cir.

1987) (noting that Rule 11 requires attorneys to make “a reasonable inquiry into the law”),

overruled on other grounds by Mars Steel Corp. v. Cont’l Bank N.A., 880 F.2d 928 (7th Cir.

1989). “Good faith is not a defense to the imposition of Rule 11 sanctions.” Ordower v.

Feldman, 826 F.2d 1569, 1574 (7th Cir. 1987).

         A court may issue monetary or nonmonetary sanctions and must ensure that such

sanction is only so great as necessary to “deter repetition of the conduct or comparable conduct

by others similarly situated.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(4). Rule 11 “does not attempt to enumerate

the factors” which courts should consider when determining appropriate sanctions. Id. advisory

committee’s note to 1993 amendment. Instead, the advisory committee’s note recognizes that

there are a variety of relevant considerations, including:

         [w]hether the improper conduct was willful, or negligent; whether it was part of a
         pattern of activity, or an isolated event; whether it infected the entire pleading, or
         only one particular count or defense; whether the person has engaged in similar
         conduct in other litigation; whether it was intended to injure; what effect it had on
         the litigation process in time or expense; whether the responsible person is trained
         in the law; what amount, given the financial resources of the responsible person,
         is needed to deter that person from repetition in the same case; [and] what amount
         is needed to deter similar activity by other litigants.

Id. Courts are “permitted to consider [the party’s] past conduct in fashioning Rule 11 sanctions.”

Vollmer v. Publishers Clearing House, 248 F.3d 698, 710 (7th Cir. 2001).

   II.      Analysis

         In his response to the Court’s order to show cause and his attached declaration, Reyes

“acknowledges” that the motion contained citations which “were inaccurate and should not have

been included without independent verification.” Resp. 2. He states that these citations were

included in his motion because he prepared a template using AI, which inserted those


                                                    3
             4:25-cv-04217-SLD-RLH                  # 33       Filed: 09/02/26          Page 4 of 11




hallucinated citations. Id. Reyes “accepts full responsibility for those errors.” Id. He requests

the Court exercise its discretion to impose no—or at worst, minimal—sanctions, arguing that (1)

he has “accepted full responsibility and implemented meaningful corrective measures,” id. at 5–

7; (2) the show cause order in Hippe was entered months after he filed the motion for default

judgment in this case, id. at 7–8; and (3) if the Court chooses to impose sanctions, it should

follow the Seventh Circuit’s reasoning in Dec v. Mullin, 171 F.4th 940 (7th Cir. 2026), and only

impose minimal ones, id. at 8–9.

         Reyes’s citation to non-existent cases and misrepresentation of others is not objectively

reasonable, and thus merits sanctions. Despite the relatively new nature of and the unique

challenges presented by AI, district courts across the Seventh Circuit have consistently and

frequently held that the inclusion of even a few hallucinated or mischaracterized cases in filings

violates Rule 11. See, e.g., Allen v. Casper, No. 25 C 10438, 2026 WL 674309, at *10 (N.D. Ill.

Mar. 10, 2026); Maldonado v. Pro. Animal Ret. Ctr., No. 1:25-CV-454-HAB-ALT, 2026 WL

925530, at *8 (N.D. Ind. Apr. 1, 2026); Virgil v. Experian Info. Sols. Inc., No. 1:25-cv-01641-

MPB-MJD, 2026 WL 401066, at *3–5 (S.D. Ind. Feb. 12, 2026). Under a few different

disciplinary vehicles, the Seventh Circuit has also repeatedly found such conduct to be worthy of

sanction. See, e.g., Dec, 2 171 F.4th at 947–48 (proceeding under Federal Rule of Appellate

Procedure 38); Perez-Castillo v. Blanche, 177 F.4th 837, 846–48 (7th Cir. 2026) (proceeding

under Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure 28 and 46); D’Ambrosio v. Meta Platforms, Inc.,

       When determining the appropriate sanction for citation errors bearing hallmark signs of

AI usage, courts consider: (1) the frequency of the errors; (2) whether the citations supported

legal standards or advanced party arguments; (3) whether counsel apologizes and takes

accountability; (4) any remedies counsel outlines to prevent similar errors in the future; (5) any

relevant prior conduct; and (6) future deterrence. Dec, 171 F.4th at 947–48; see also Perez-

Castillo, 177 F.4th at 846–49; Mid. Cent. Operating Eng’rs Health & Welfare Fund v.

HoosierVac LLC, No. 2:24-cv-00326-JPH-MJD, 2025 WL 1511211, at *1–2 (S.D. Ind. May 28,

2025). When courts find counsel’s contrition persuasive and the errors are minor, they tend to

issue nonmonetary sanctions, see, e.g., Dec, 171 F.4th at 947–48 (admonishing counsel for

including AI-hallucinated citations and quotations but declining to impose any additional

consequences), or forego sanctions altogether, Hippe v. Retention Brands, LLC, No. 25-cv-596,

2026 WL 2043246, at *1 (E.D. Wis. July 15, 2026) (finding that Reyes’s decision to withdraw

any request for attorneys’ fees constituted sufficient consequences). However, when courts are

not so persuaded, there is evidence of prior improper conduct, or the errors are significant, courts

have instead imposed monetary sanctions. See, e.g., Perez-Castillo, 177 F.4th at 848–49

(imposing a $5,000 sanction on one attorney who submitted a brief containing various AI

hallucinations and, after being ordered to fix all citation errors, re-entered the brief with several

of the same hallucinated quotations, and referring the other, who admitted to preparing both

versions of the brief with assistance from ChatGPT and without further cite checking but

expressed great remorse, to the state attorney disciplinary authorities); Mid Cent. Operating,

2025 WL 1511211, at *2 (imposing a $6,000 sanction on an attorney because he filed three

separate briefs containing non-existent AI-generated citations and “the penalties imposed on

other attorneys for similar misconduct have evidently failed to act as a deterrent” (quotation

marks omitted)).

       In this case, significant and monetary sanctions are appropriate not because the citation

errors themselves were particularly egregious—the motion included just four citation errors, see

July 24, 2026 Order 11 (discussing Mot. Default J. 4–6), and although the citations advanced

Cole’s argument, each of the arguments they supported are generally accepted, see Mot. Default

J. 4–6—but because of the context in which Reyes engaged in this conduct. It is true that Reyes

takes full responsibility for the citation errors and does not attempt to minimize the severity of

these errors. See generally Resp. In fact, Reyes’s response to the order to show cause is, at first

review, quite compelling. He admits that he “conducted legal research and relied in part on prior

work product developed with the help of AI in similar ADA Title III matters, as a drafting aid

and template,” and “recognizes that while AI and templates may serve as drafting aids, the same

can never substitute for independently locating, reviewing, and verifying every authority cited in

a pleading.” Id. at 3. He further emphasizes that these errors were not committed knowingly but

acknowledges “that the absence of such intent does not excuse the failure to independently verify

the authorities before filing.” Id. at 4. He also identifies several preventative measures that he is

already taking to ensure such errors do not occur in the future. Id. at 4, 6–7.

       Reyes’s contrition unravels, however, upon closer examination. This is at least the third

case in which he has filed briefings with AI hallucinations. In October 2025, he filed a nearly

identical motion for default judgment in Hippe which included the same faulty citations. See

Mot. Default J., Hippe, No. 25-cv-596, ECF No. 11. An order to show cause was not entered in

that case until June 23, 2026. See generally Hippe, 2026 WL 1802971.

        Therefore, the Court finds it appropriate to impose a monetary sanction of $1,000 and to

require Reyes to send a copy of this order to the Illinois ARDC. The monetary sanction of

$1,000 is sufficient but no greater than necessary to “deter repetition of the conduct or

comparable conduct by others similarly situated.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(4). A $1,000 sanction,

while not insignificant, is modest relative to other recent sanctions for similar citation errors.

See, e.g., Mid. Cent. Operating, 2025 WL 1511211, at *1–2 (imposing a $6,000 sanction upon

counsel who filed “briefs containing non-existent AI-generated legal citations on three separate

occasions and the penalties imposed on other attorneys for similar misconduct have evidently

failed to act as a deterrent” (quotation marks omitted)); Allen, 2026 WL 674309, at *10

(imposing a $1,500 sanction upon a pro se litigant whose filing included at least two hallucinated

cases and false statements of law); Davis, 2026 WL 1408448, at *6 (imposing a $2,000 sanction

upon counsel who filed one brief containing two hallucinated citations); Perez-Castillo, 177

F.4th at 847–48 (imposing a $5,000 sanction under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 46 upon

counsel who filed a brief containing several AI hallucinations and, after being put on notice of

the deficiencies, resubmitted the brief with at least two of the prior hallucinated quotations still

included). However, the Court finds it appropriate given the circumstances of this case. The

Court is unconvinced by Reyes’s apology and proposed corrective measures; finds that his

relevant prior conduct supports and deterrence is served by a monetary sanction; and finds that

the purpose of the improper citations in advancing party arguments cancels out the mitigating

nature of the relatively small number of citation errors. Nevertheless, the Court recognizes that
