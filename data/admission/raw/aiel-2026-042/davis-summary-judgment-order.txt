                              II. LEGAL STANDARD

       A district court shall grant summary judgment "if the movant shows that

 there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to

 judgment as a matter of law." Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). A genuine dispute of material

 fact exists if "the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict

 for the nonmoving party." Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248

 (1986). The party seeking summary judgment has the burden of establishing

 that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact. See Celotex Corp. v.

 Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). To survive summary judgment, the opposing

 party must go beyond the pleadings and identify specific facts showing the

 existence of a genuine issue for trial. See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 256.

       The Court construes all facts in the light most favorable to the nonmoving

 party, giving the nonmoving party the benefit of all reasonable inferences. See

 Chaib v. Geo Grp., Inc., 819 F.3d 337, 341 (7th Cir. 2016). The Court does not

 weigh the evidence, judge credibility, or determine the truth of the matter, but

 rather determines only whether a genuine issue of triable fact exists. See Nat'l

                                         7
Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD     Document 134 Filed 09/28/26       Page 8 of 23 PageID #:
                                    <pageID>



 Athletic Sportswear, Inc. v. Westfield Ins. Co., 528 F.3d 508, 512 (7th Cir. 2008).

 While the Court will "construe all facts and draw all reasonable inferences in the

 nonmoving party's favor," the moving party may prevail at summary judgment

 "'by showing an absence of evidence to support the nonmoving party's claims.'"

 Lewis v. Ind. Wesleyan Univ., 36 F.4th 755, 759 (7th Cir. 2022) (quoting Tyburski

 v. City of Chicago, 964 F.3d 590, 597 (7th Cir. 2020)).

                                  III. DISCUSSION

       Title VII forbids an employer from discriminating against any individual

 with respect to his or her "compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of

 employment, because of such individual's . . . sex." 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1).

 The legal standard in a Title VII case is holistic: "whether the evidence would

 permit a reasonable factfinder to conclude that the plaintiff's race, ethnicity, sex,

 religion, or other proscribed factor caused the discharge or other adverse

 employment action." Burton v. Will Cnty. Sheriff's Merit Comm'n, 176 F.4th 544,

 548 (7th Cir. 2026) (citing Ortiz v. Werner Enters., Inc., 834 F.3d 760, 765 (7th

 Cir. 2016)). The burden-shifting approach in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green,

 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973), is "one way of culling the relevant evidence" to answer

 this question. Johnson v. Advoc. Health & Hosps. Corp., 892 F.3d 887, 894 (7th

 Cir. 2018) ("[T]he well-known and oft-used McDonnell Douglas framework for

 evaluating discrimination remains an efficient way to organize, present, and

 assess evidence in discrimination cases.").

       Davis relies primarily on Ortiz to present her claim. But JDC's motion is

 organized around the McDonnell Douglas framework. So the Court will use that

Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD      Document 134 Filed 09/28/26        Page 9 of 23 PageID #:
                                     <pageID>



 approach first and circle back to Ortiz for good measure. See Tyburski, 964 F.3d

 at 599 ("As long as the court considers the evidence as a whole, it may also use

 the McDonnell Douglas framework as a supplemental tool."). Under either

 framework, the undisputed evidence considered as a whole shows that JDC is

 entitled to summary judgment. 1

 A.    McDonnell Douglas Burden-Shifting Approach

       Under the McDonnell Douglas framework, to make out a prima facie case

 of discrimination in the failure-to-hire context, a plaintiff must establish that: (1)

 she was a member of a protected class; (2) she was qualified for and applied to

 an open position; (3) she was rejected; and (4) the employer filled the position by

 hiring someone outside the protected class, or left the position open. Oliver v.

 Joint Logistics Managers, Inc., 893 F.3d 408, 412 (7th Cir. 2018) (citing Blise v.

 Antaramian, 409 F.3d 861, 866 (7th Cir. 2005)).

       If a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case, the burden shifts to the

 employer to "articulate some legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason" for the

 adverse action. McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802. If the employer can

 articulate such a reason, the burden then shifts back to the plaintiff to

 demonstrate that the employer's proffered reason is pretextual. Igasaki v. Illinois

       1.    Prima facie showing under McDonnell Douglas is satisfied

       At this stage, there is no dispute that the first, third, and fourth prongs

 are met. Davis is a woman and thus a member of a protected class. She was

 rejected for the DSYS position in 2023 while Godwin King, male, was instead

 selected. Dkt. 118 at 14. As to the second prong, JDC argues that Davis was not

 qualified for the position because it sought an internal candidate and because of

 an alleged informal policy of not rehiring employees who had been fired in the

 past year. Dkt. 121 at 19. JDC, however, concedes that Davis was "qualifi[ed] on

 paper." Id. Viewing the facts in a light most favorable to Davis, a reasonable fact

 finder could determine that she was qualified for the position. She had over

 twenty years of experience with JDC and over six years of experience in the same

 role she sought. Her prima facie showing is therefore met.

       2.    Legitimate nondiscriminatory reason

       JDC proffers a legitimate nondiscriminatory reason for not rehiring Davis.

 At this stage, JDC "only has to produce admissible evidence that would permit a

 rational jury to conclude that the employment decision had not been motivated

 by discriminatory animus." Stockwell v. City of Harvey, 597 F.3d 895, 903 (7th

 Cir. 2010). This is a light burden. Id. (citing Pilditch v. Bd. of Educ. of City of

 Chicago, 3 F.3d 1113, 1117 (7th Cir. 1993)). Reasons for not rehiring can be

 subjective. So long as JDC points to "reasonably specific facts that explain how

 it formed its opinion," it has met its burden. Oliver, 893 F.3d at 413 (quoting U.S.

 E.E.O.C. v. Target Corp., 460 F.3d 946, 957 (7th Cir. 2006) (describing the

 employer's burden when it relies on subjective reasons for not hiring)). Here, this

 standard is easily satisfied.

       Only ten months had passed between that CAR and when Davis applied

 for her old position. JDC maintains that Davis was not rehired because of her

 as a result. Dkt. 118 at 17–18. JDC notes it had "more insights into Plaintiff's

 not to rehire Davis for the same reasons it terminated her in the first place. The

 record shows there was a breakdown in trust between Davis and JDC. A

 reasonable jury could find that the nondiscriminatory factors that drove Davis's

 termination similarly drove JDC's decision not to rehire her. See Burton v. Will

 Cnty. Sheriff's Merit Comm'n, 176 F.4th 544, 548 (7th Cir. 2026) (a negative

 employment reference is unquestionably a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason

 for failure to hire). The burden now shifts back to Davis to show pretext.

       3.     Pretext

       Once the employer has articulated a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason

 for its decision, the presumption of discrimination falls away. Cianci v. Pettibone

 Corp., 152 F.3d 723, 726 (7th Cir. 1998). The plaintiff then has the burden of

                                         11
Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD     Document 134 Filed 09/28/26      Page 12 of 23 PageID
                                   #: <pageID>



 producing sufficient evidence to show that the proffered reason is a pretext for

 discrimination. Id.; see McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802, 804.

       Pretext means "a dishonest explanation, a lie rather than an oddity or an

 error." Kulumani v. Blue Cross Blue Shield Ass'n, 224 F.3d 681, 685 (7th Cir.

 2000). The question is not whether the employer's explanation for its

 employment decision was "accurate, wise, or well-considered," but whether the

 employer's explanation was "honest." Ptasznik v. St. Joseph Hosp., 464 F.3d 691,

 696 (7th Cir. 2006) (quoting Stewart v. Henderson, 207 F.3d 374, 378 (7th Cir.

 2000)). Put differently, an otherwise legitimate, non-discriminatory hiring

 rationale is pretextual if the hiring manager did not "sincerely believe[]" the

 rationale when making his final decision. Little v. Ill. Dep't of Revenue, 369 F.3d

 1007, 1015 (7th Cir. 2004). "Pretext can be proven, among other ways, by

 evidence (1) that the employer's explanation has no basis in fact; (2) of

 ambiguous or suggestive comments or conduct; or (3) of better treatment of

 people similarly situated but for the protected characteristic." Paterakos v. City

 of Chicago, 147 F.4th 787, 797 (7th Cir. 2025) (cleaned up).

       Importantly, "a showing of pretext alone is not enough; the plaintiff must

 also show that the explanations are a pretext for the prohibited animus."

 Hitchcock v. Angel Corps., Inc., 718 F.3d 733, 740 (7th Cir. 2013) (emphasis

 added). A plaintiff can demonstrate that the employer's explanations are

 pretextual either directly, by showing that "a discriminatory reason more likely

 motivated" the employer's actions, or indirectly, by showing that the employer's

 explanations are "unworthy of credence." Senske v. Sybase, Inc., 588 F.3d 501,

                                         12
Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD       Document 134 Filed 09/28/26     Page 13 of 23 PageID
                                     #: <pageID>



 507 (7th Cir. 2009). To show that the employer's non-discriminatory

 explanations are not credible, the plaintiff must point to evidence that the

 employer's stated reasons are not the real reasons for the employer's action, have

 no grounding in fact, or are insufficient to warrant the employer's decision. Id.;

 Boumehdi v. Plastag Holdings, LLC, 489 F.3d 781, 792 (7th Cir. 2007) (noting

 that a plaintiff must identify such "weaknesses, implausibilities, inconsistencies,

 or contradictions" in the employer's asserted reasons that a reasonable person

 could find them not credible). "Pretext does not exist if the decisionmaker

 honestly believed the nondiscriminatory reason given by an employer for an

 adverse employment action." Downing v. Abbott Lab'ys, 48 F.4th 793, 804–05

 (7th Cir. 2022) (cleaned up).

       At bottom, Davis must "demonstrat[e] by a preponderance of evidence that

 the stated reasons for her [not being rehired] were false, not that they were unfair

 or baseless." Anderson v. Street, 104 F.4th 646, 653 (7th Cir. 2024).

       To show pretext, Davis points to Alex Murdock as an example of a similarly

 situated male who was treated more favorably. Davis then points to an alleged

 pattern of males being treated more favorably than females by JDC. She also

 notes that Kervan's informal policy of not rehiring within a year of termination

 was selectively enforced and provides examples using comparators. See Khowaja

 v. Sessions, 893 F.3d 1010, 1015 (7th Cir. 2018) ("[C]omparator evidence and

 selective enforcement of an employer's rules are relevant" to a pretext analysis.).

 Lastly, Davis argues that a reasonable jury could choose to believe that JDC's

 "shifting explanations" evince pretext. Dkt. 120 at 17. The Court takes each in

             a.     Male comparator

       "When using comparators to demonstrate pretext, a plaintiff must usually

 'show that the comparators (1) dealt with the same supervisor, (2) were subject

 to the same standards, and (3) engaged in similar conduct without such

 differentiating or mitigating circumstances as would distinguish their conduct

 or the employer's treatment of them.'" Napier v. Orchard Sch. Found., 137 F.4th

 884, 894 (7th Cir. 2025), cert. denied, 146 S. Ct. 328 (2025) (cleaned up) (quoting

 Coleman v. Donahoe, 667 F.3d 835, 846 (7th Cir. 2012)).

       The purpose of the "similarly situated inquiry is to eliminate other possible

 explanatory variables, . . . [to] isolate the critical independent variable—

 discriminatory animus." Mitchell v. Exxon Mobil Corp., 143 F.4th 800, 810 (7th

 Cir. 2025) (alterations in original) (quoting Smith v. City of Janesville, 40 F.4th

 816, 823 (7th Cir. 2022)). In other words, the plaintiff must establish that "the

 purported comparator was 'directly comparable to her in all material respects'

 so as to 'eliminate other possible explanatory variables.'" Id. (quoting Gamble v.

 Cnty. of Cook, 106 F.4th 622, 626 (7th Cir. 2024)).

       Alex Murdock, a male, is Davis's proffered comparator. Dkt. 113-10 at 4;

 Dkt. 120 at 8. JDC employed Murdock from 2015 to 2017. After receiving two

 CARs, he was eventually terminated in 2017, dkt. 120 at 4–5; dkt. 113-10 at 3,

 for poor work performance, insubordination or failure to follow instruction, and

 neglect or failure to perform assigned duties, dkt. 113-13 at 5; dkt. 120 at 8; dkt.


                                         14
Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD     Document 134 Filed 09/28/26      Page 15 of 23 PageID
                                   #: <pageID>



 121 at 18. In March 2022, more than four years after he was fired, JDC hired

 Murdock as the second shift DSYS. Dkt. 120 at 5; Dkt. 113-10 at 3.

       Davis argues Murdock is similarly situated because both Murdock and

 Davis were involuntarily terminated by Kervan, but only Murdock was rehired.

 Dkt. 120 at 5; Dkt. 123 at 4. Davis maintains that a reasonable juror could find

 that JDC's stated excuse for rehiring Murdock but not Davis is contrived and

 suspicious and thus merely a pretext for the real basis for their different

 treatment: sex discrimination. Dkt. 120 at 10, 12. JDC disagrees, arguing that

 Murdock and Davis are not similarly situated because Murdock had been

 terminated more than four years prior to his rehiring. Dkt. 118 at 16.

       The Court agrees with JDC that Davis and Murdock are not similarly

 situated. Similarly situated individuals must be similarly situated in all material

 respects in order to "eliminate other possible explanatory variables." Mitchell,

 143 F.4th at 810. Moreover, they must be subject to the same standards when

 being considered for rehiring.

       Davis was fired and then applied for her same position ten months later.

 Conversely, Murdock was fired and was rehired more than four years later. That

 four-year difference matters. Ten months is an insufficient time for the dust to

 settle after a termination based on divisive behavior. A more than four-year gap

 is a different story. What's more, nowhere in the record does it show that

 Murdock engaged in divisive behavior that alienated himself from his peers.

 These "other possible explanatory variables" explain the different treatment JDC

 gave to Davis and Murdock, Mitchell, 143 F.4th at 810, not sex discrimination.

 was terminated after the September 2022 incident. While Davis disputes the

 veracity of the reasons given for her termination, dkt. 120 at 8, it is undisputed

 that the reasons were given and are detailed in the CAR. JDC's legitimate

 nondiscriminatory reason for not rehiring Davis was that she was recently

 terminated and thus was not considered for rehire. None of the above evidence

 gives rise to an inference that JDC, when it decided not to rehire Davis, did so

 because of her sex.

             c.    Kervan's selective enforcement of JDC's informal
                   rehiring policy

       Davis argues that JDC's informal policy of not rehiring individuals fired

 less than one year previously is simply "false." Dkt. 120 at 9. Davis points to

 three examples of employees that she alleges were fired but then rehired within

 one year: Makalya Granson (female), Serena Thompson (female), and

 Christopher Sutton (male). Dkt. 113-20 at 3.

       Davis contends that Granson was fired and rehired by Kervan less than a

 year later. Id. JDC disputes this and maintains that Granson was not rehired

 within one year of termination and instead was rehired more than two years after

 she was terminated. Dkt. 121 at 4.

Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD     Document 134 Filed 09/28/26     Page 18 of 23 PageID
                                   #: <pageID>



       Davis says that Thompson threatened to run over another employee, was

 allowed to resign in lieu of termination, yet was rehired by Kervan a few weeks

 later. Dkt. 113-20 at 3. JDC disputes this and argues that Thompson voluntarily

 resigned from her position in March 2017 and was rehired in September 2018.

 Dkt. 121 at 3.

       Davis then points to Sutton, who was fired for attendance issues in August

 of 2022, but rehired mere months later in November of 2022. Dkt. 113-20 at 3;

 Dkt. 121 at 12. JDC concedes Sutton as an "exception" to JDC's informal rule,

 and notes that his original termination was because of attendance issues and

 not for misconduct or interpersonal problems. Dkt. 121 at 12. JDC further

 argues that Sutton was demoted to an entry level position upon his return, and

 thus the job he was rehired for was a different position from the one he was fired

 from. Id.

       As to Granson and Thompson, these factual disputes are immaterial.

 Thompson and Granson are both women. Assuming the facts in a light most

 favorable to Davis and that these employees were rehired less than a year after

 their termination, this evidence shows JDC made exceptions to its one-year

 rehire policy in favor of women. It is not evidence the one-year policy is a sham

 to cover up JDC's refusal to rehire Davis because she was a woman.

       As to Sutton, no reasonable jury could infer pretext based on the

 undisputed facts. There is a material difference between Sutton and Davis.

 Sutton returned to JDC in an entry level position, a demotion from the position

 he was terminated from. In contrast, Davis applied for a supervisory position—

Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD     Document 134 Filed 09/28/26       Page 19 of 23 PageID
                                   #: <pageID>



 the same position she was fired from. No reasonable jury could find that JDC

 discriminated against Davis on the basis of her sex because it effectively demoted

 Sutton but did not rehire Davis for her supervisory position.

       Finally, Davis also provides a declaration by James Snider, who was

 employed by JDC from 2014–2021 and was Kervan's subordinate. Dkt. 113-19.

 This affidavit does not establish pretext. Snider "had extensive dealings with

 Kervan and observed that she had a pattern of treating male employees more

 favorably than female employees." Dkt. 120 at 10. Specifically, Snider claims

 that Kervan "favored black males over black females." Dkt. 113-19 ¶ 21. But the

 rehiring of Granson and Thompson suggest otherwise. Moreover, Snider's

 affidavit also details an example of a female employee who committed a number

 of terminable offenses who received favorable treatment from Kervan. Dkt. 113-

 19 ¶ 28–30. In other words, Snider's affidavit establishes that Kervan's

 preferences, not sex discrimination, explains who is hired, fired, and rehired at

 JDC: "If [Kervan] liked you, she took care of you, if she didn’t like you, she would

 discipline or terminate you." Dkt. 113-19 ¶ 27. This is fundamentally

 inconsistent with Davis's argument that the one-year rehiring policy was a

 pretext for sex discrimination.

             d.     Shifting explanations

       Davis's final argument for pretext is JDC's "shifting explanations" for not

 rehiring her. Dkt. 120 at 17. JDC informed the EEOC that Davis was "not

 qualified" for the position. Dkt. 113-14 at 7. But now JDC argues that while

 Davis was qualified "on paper," "the facts surrounding [Davis's] termination

                                         19
Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD    Document 134 Filed 09/28/26     Page 20 of 23 PageID
                                  #: <pageID>



 made her unqualified for the DSYS position in August 2023." Dkt. 121 at 19.

 Davis seizes on this inconsistency as proof that JDC's so-called practice of

 "deeming former employees who had been fired a lengthier time ago as eligible

 for rehire but not those employees who had been fired more recently" is

 "implausible on its face and may be disbelieved by a jury." Dkt. 120 at 13.

       This argument overstates JDC's inconsistent reasoning. While an

 employer's shifting factual accounts and explanations for adverse employment

 action "can often support a reasonable inference" of pretext, Donley v. Stryker

 Sales Corp., 906 F.3d 635, 638 (7th Cir. 2018), it is also true that "[m]erely

 providing multiple, or additional, reasons for an adverse employment decision

 does not establish pretext." Saud v. DePaul Univ., 154 F.4th 563, 569 (7th Cir.

 2025). "[T]o give rise to pretext, 'explanations must actually be shifting and

 inconsistent.'" Id. (quoting Bagwe v. Sedgwick Claims Mgmt. Servs., Inc., 811

 F.3d 866, 881 (7th Cir. 2016)).

       At bottom, JDC's explanations for not rehiring a recently fired Davis are

 not inconsistent with one another. They all center around Davis's recent

 termination, the reasons for that departure, and JDC's and/or Kervan's general

 lack of desire to rehire her. So long as an employer "honestly believed it made

 the correct employment decision—even if its decision was inaccurate, unfair,

 foolish, trivial, or baseless—[the] claims cannot succeed." Barnes-Staples v.

 Carnahan, 88 F.4th 712, 716 (7th Cir. 2023) (cleaned up); Upchurch v. Indiana,

 146 F.4th 579, 587 (7th Cir. 2025); see also Hill v. Tangherlini, 724 F.3d 965,

 968 (7th Cir. 2013) ("[W]e evaluate the honesty of the employer's explanation,

                                        20
Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD     Document 134 Filed 09/28/26       Page 21 of 23 PageID
                                   #: <pageID>



 rather than its validity or reasonableness . . . ."). And it is not enough that JDC

 offered different explanations in the EEOC charge and in depositions. See Saud,

 154 F.4th at 569     ("'Where an employer relies on multiple reasons for the

 termination, its failure to address all of the reasons in each communication

 about the employee is not enough to show contradictions or shifts in rationales

 that suggest pretext.'" (emphasis in original) (quoting Castro v. DeVry Univ., Inc.,

 786 F.3d 559, 577 (7th Cir. 2015)). On this record, no reasonable jury could find

 that JDC's legitimate nondiscriminatory reasons for not rehiring Davis were

 pretext for sex discrimination.

       In sum, no reasonable jury could find under the McDonnell Douglas

 framework that JDC discriminated against Davis based on her sex when it

 decided not to rehire her.

 B.    Ortiz's Holistic Approach

       Davis's claim fares no better under Ortiz. Davis points to the "pile of

 evidence," dkt. 120 at 8–11, largely stated above, and argues it demonstrates a

 triable issue of fact for a jury to infer pretext and thus precludes summary

 judgment. The Court disagrees.

       The undisputed facts show Davis was terminated in September of 2022

 because JDC lost trust in her due to her disruptive and divisive behavior and

 failure to follow JDC policy. Yet she applied for the same position less than one

 year later. JDC chose not to hire her for the same reasons it terminated her.




                                         21
Case 1:24-cv-01918-JRO-MJD      Document 134 Filed 09/28/26        Page 22 of 23 PageID
                                    #: <pageID>



       The record lacks evidence to support a finding that Davis's sex had

 anything to do with the decision. Even assuming the selective enforcement of the

 one-year rehiring policy, Davis's own evidence shows that JDC's decision not to

 rehire Davis was based on the favoritism exhibited by Kervan for certain

 employees over others. Dkt. 113-19 ¶ 27. But favoritism and dysfunction are not

 actionable under Title VII. See Blise v. Antaramian, 409 F.3d 861, 867 (7th Cir.

 2005) ("[A]n employer's decision to favor one candidate over another can be

 mistaken, ill-considered or foolish, but so long as the employer honestly believed

 those reasons, pretext has not been shown." (cleaned up)). As JDC puts it,

 "[Davis] was no longer a good fit in September 2022, and nothing demonstrably

 changed between then and July 31, 2023 . . . ." Dkt. 118 at 15. So long as an

 employer "honestly believed it made the correct employment decision—even if its

 decision was inaccurate, unfair, foolish, trivial, or baseless—[the] claims cannot

 succeed." Barnes-Staples, 88 F.4th at 716 (cleaned up); Upchurch, 146 F.4th at

 587; see cf., Napier, 137 F.4th at 895–96 ("No reasonable fact-finder could

 determine that [the plaintiff's filing of] litigation, rather than the events that led

 to his termination, was the 'but-for' cause of [the employer's] decision not to

 rehire him" where plaintiff was terminated because employer "no longer trusted

 him.").

       In the end, under Ortiz's holistic inquiry, no reasonable jury would dispute

 that the circumstances of Davis's termination, not sex-based animus, drove

 JDC's decision not to rehire her.
