                         NONPRECEDENTIAL OPINION

LARSON, Judge

      Appellant Michael Kwabena Asiedu challenges the district court’s decision to

dismiss his complaint under Minn. R. Civ. P. 12.02(e) and impose sanctions. We affirm.

                                        FACTS

      In May 2025, Asiedu filed an amended complaint naming respondents Olmsted

County’s Health, Housing, and Human Services division (the county), Teri Eastlund
(caseworker Eastlund), Kris Giere (supervisor Giere), and John Does 1-5 1 as defendants.

The county is a governmental subdivision “that administers child protection services.”

Caseworker Eastlund and supervisor Giere are county employees working in “child

protection services.”    John Does 1-5 are “other county employees or agents who

participated in the acts alleged.”

       According to the complaint, 2 respondents removed Asiedu’s 12-year-old child

(child) from school without a court order, child-in-need-of-protection-or-services petition,

or emergency-removal findings.       The complaint asserts that respondents’ reason for

removing child was “false and unsubstantiated claims” that child “had disclosed abuse.”

Respondents placed child in protective care. Caseworker Eastlund then authored a safety

plan (the plan), which child signed. The plan contained the “false and unsubstantiated”

abuse allegations. The plan was disseminated to third parties. Because of the plan, Asiedu

lost access to child’s education and health information, had restricted access to child, and

was not able to make decisions about child’s schooling or health. Respondents then refused

to give Asiedu access to the plan, case notes, or other relevant records. Based upon these

allegations, Asiedu asserted five claims against respondents, including a claim under

42 U.S.C. § 1983 (2025), and four state-law claims.

       B.     Official and Vicarious Official Immunity

       Asiedu challenges the district court’s decision to dismiss his state-law claims on the

grounds that employees were entitled to common-law official immunity and the county

was entitled to common-law vicarious official immunity. Common-law official immunity

provides that a public official cannot be held personally liable for damages from carrying

out “duties which call for the exercise of [their] judgment or discretion.” Elwood, 423

N.W.2d at 677 (quotation omitted). Official immunity protects individual government

actors so they may “perform their duties effectively, without fear of personal liability that

might inhibit the exercise of their independent judgment.” Kariniemi v. City of Rockford,

882 N.W.2d 593, 600 (Minn. 2016) (quotation omitted).

       “Official immunity is subject to two limitations.” Jepsen as Tr. for Dean v. County

of Pope, 966 N.W.2d 472, 482 (Minn. 2021). First, a public official is not entitled to

official immunity if their “exercise of independent [judgment] or discretion is a willful or

malicious wrong—meaning that the public official intentionally performs a wrongful act

without legal justification or excuse or willfully violates a known right of the plaintiff.” Id.

Second, a public official is not entitled to official immunity if they “fail[] to perform or

negligently perform[] an act that is deemed ministerial.” Id. (quotation omitted).


                                               7
       Asiedu first argues, without citation to any authority, that the allegations in his

complaint sufficiently allege “willful or malicious” conduct on the part of employees. In

this context, “wil[l]ful and malicious are synonymous” and mean “the intentional doing of

a wrongful act without legal justification or excuse, or, otherwise stated, the willful

violation of a known right.” Rico v. State, 472 N.W.2d 100, 107 (Minn. 1991). And, as

observed by the district court, there are no allegations in the complaint that employees

acted with an improper motive in their efforts to respond to an allegation of child abuse—

even assuming as true that the allegation was “false and unsubstantiated.”

       Asiedu argues second, again without citation to any authority, that employees were

not acting in “an open-ended discretionary function” because they failed to follow the

proper statutory process for removing a child from a parent’s care. Common-law official

immunity applies to a public official’s “discretionary” duties, but not their “ministerial”

duties. Anderson v. Anoka Hennepin Indep. Sch. Dist. 11, 678 N.W.2d 651, 655 (Minn.

2004) (quotation omitted). To determine whether an act is ministerial or discretionary, we

focus “on the nature of the act at issue.” Id. at 656. “A discretionary act requires the

exercise of individual judgment.” Kari v. City of Maplewood, 582 N.W.2d 921, 923 (Minn.

1998). Whereas a ministerial duty is “absolute, certain, and imperative, involving merely

the execution of a specific duty arising from fixed and designated facts.” Johnson v. State,

553 N.W.2d 40, 46 (Minn. 1996) (quotation omitted).

       With respect to employees, Asiedu alleged that, in their role working for “child

protection services,” they placed child in protective custody, created the plan based on

fabricated allegations, disseminated the plan to third parties, and denied Asiedu access to


                                             8
relevant information. We conclude that these are discretionary duties. See Olson v.

Ramsey County, 509 N.W.2d 368, 371-72 (Minn. 1993) (concluding county worker

exercised discretionary duties when making case plan). And in our independent review,

we find no support for Asiedu’s assertion that, because he alleged the discretionary conduct

did not comport with existing law, employees are not entitled to official immunity. See,

e.g., Elwood, 423 N.W.2d at 676-79 (concluding police officers were entitled to official

immunity even though they unlawfully entered home without warrant).

       Moving to the county, if common-law official immunity applies to a public official’s

conduct, the government employer generally will be vicariously immune from suit as well.

See Schroeder v. St. Louis County, 708 N.W.2d 497, 508 (Minn. 2006). “Ultimately, the

extension of vicarious official immunity is a policy question for the court.” Id. Even so,

granting official immunity to an employee while denying it to their employer would put

“stifling attention on the social worker’s performance, to the serious detriment of that

performance.” Olson, 509 N.W.2d at 372. Because employees were entitled to official

immunity, we conclude that the district court correctly extended the same protection to the

county.

       For these reasons, we conclude the district court correctly dismissed Asiedu’s state-

law claims.

                                             II.

       Asiedu challenges the district court’s decision to impose sanctions using its inherent

power and to award respondents attorney fees. District courts possess inherent authority

to impose sanctions to protect “their vital function—the disposition of individual cases to


                                             9
deliver remedies for wrongs and justice freely and without purchase; completely and

without denial; promptly and without delay, conformable to the laws.” Patton v. Newmar

Corp., 538 N.W.2d 116, 118 (Minn. 1995) (quotations omitted). We review a district

court’s decision to use its inherent authority to impose sanctions in the form of attorney

fees for an abuse of discretion. Peterson v. 2004 Ford Crown Victoria, 792 N.W.2d 454,

461 (Minn. App. 2010). “Attorney fees may be an appropriate sanction when a party has

‘acted in bad faith, vexatiously, wantonly, or for oppressive reasons.’” Id. at 462 (quoting

Chambers v. NASCO, Inc., 501 U.S. 32, 45-46 (1991)). A party challenging a district

court’s decision to impose attorney fees using its inherent authority “has the difficult

burden of convincing” our court that “‘no reasonable person’” would agree with the

imposition of sanctions. Patton, 538 N.W.2d at 119 (quoting Marrocco v. Gen. Motors

Corp., 966 F.2d 220, 223 (7th Cir. 1992)).

       Here, the district court found that Asiedu repeatedly misrepresented caselaw, either

by citing a case for the wrong legal principle or citing non-existent cases. And during the

hearing, Asiedu admitted that he used artificial intelligence as a research tool to draft his

filings and did not ensure the accuracy of the caselaw citations on the basis that he was

“doing this part time.” The district court determined that, in doing so, Asiedu did not

conduct himself in accordance with the promises he made to the court when signing his

documents. Cf. Minn. R. Civ. P. 11.02(b) (stating that when signing pleadings, attorney

certifies “the claims, defenses, and other legal contentions therein are warranted by existing

law.”); Dehghani v. Castro, No. 2:25-CV-0052, 2025 WL 988009, at *6 (D.N.M. Apr. 2,

2025) (finding a Fed. R. Civ. P. 11 violation for, in part, citing to non-existent case law


                                             10
because the citations “do not support the stated proposition.”), aff’d, 2025 WL 1361765

(D.N.M. May 9, 2025); Ramirez v. Humala, No. 24-CV-242, 2025 WL 1384161, at *1

(E.D.N.Y. May 13, 2025) (listing multiple district court decisions and a Second Circuit

case finding “submission of nonexistent case citations in filings to the court” as amounting

to a Fed. R. Civ. P. 11 violation). 10 The district court also concluded the citations misled

the district court. See Matthew Garner v. Kadince, Inc., 571 P.3d 812, 815 (Utah Ct. App.

2025) (“A fake opinion is not ‘existing law’ that can support a party’s legal contention.”

(citation omitted)).

       Based upon this record, we conclude the district court found that Asiedu acted in

bad faith when he did not ensure the accuracy of the caselaw citations on the basis that he

was “doing this part time.” We hold pro se parties to the same standard as attorneys when

filing documents in court. See Fitzgerald v. Fitzgerald, 629 N.W.2d 115, 119 (Minn. App.

2001) (“[P]ro se litigants are generally held to the same standards as attorneys and must

comply with court rules.”). Accordingly, we conclude Asiedu has not met his burden to

show the district court abused its discretion when it imposed sanctions. See Patton, 538

N.W.2d at 119.
