                                  13                                           INTRODUCTION

                                  14          Lawyers owe a duty of candor to the Court, a duty that cannot be met without reading and

                                  15   understanding the legal authorities on which their arguments rely. When lawyers fail to check

                                  16   citations in a brief prior to filing, as discussed at length previously, [Dkt. 161], or when they

                                  17   materially misrepresent the facts or holdings in a case, they breach their professional and ethical

                                  18   obligations. Moreover, managerial and senior lawyers have professional and ethical duties to the

                                  19   Court, their clients, and their employees to appropriately supervise the work performed. See Cal.

                                  20   Rules of Prof. Conduct r. 5.1. At minimum, a supervising lawyer should read and understand the

                                  21   content of all pleadings and check citations to ensure their accuracy. Managers in law firms have

                                  22   an obligation to take reasonable steps to ensure all lawyers in the firm make ethical representations

                                  23   to the Court.

                                  24          As previously discussed in the Order sanctioning Attorney Katherine Cervantes, [Dkt. 161],

                                  25   courts have long admonished lawyers for their willful or reckless citations to nonexistent or

                                  26   improper case law and fictitious statutes in briefs. See, e.g., Willy v. Coastal Corp., 855 F.2d 1160,

                                  27   1172 (5th Cir. 1988) (affirming Rule 11 sanctions for, among other things, “citing to nonexistent

                                  28   rules of law”). When submitting filings to a court, one of a lawyer’s basic professional and ethical
                                       Case 3:23-cv-06558-PHK            Document 230          Filed 04/28/26      Page 2 of 17




                                   1   duties is not to “present a claim or defense in litigation that is not warranted under existing law”

                                   2   regardless of the source of the alleged existing law (whether from a computerized source, a

                                   3   generative AI, a traditional legal research tool such as Westlaw, or a treatise). See Cal. Rules of

                                   4   Prof. Conduct r. 3.1(a)(2). Otherwise, “pleadings that cite fake legal authority undermine[] the rule

                                   5   of law and a judicial system based on reason. Such fakery also erodes public trust in the legal

                                   6   profession.” In re Kenney, 422 So. 3d 905, 924 (La. Ct. App. 2025). Supervisors who do not draft

                                   7   or sign submissions to the court, but who remain counsel of record and fail to question the accuracy

                                   8   of defective pleadings, fail in their duty of supervision. Mattox v. Prod. Innovations Rsch., LLC,

                                   9   807 F. Supp. 3d 1341, 1351 (E.D. Okla. Oct. 22, 2025).

                                  10           Here, there is no dispute that the lawyers at issue submitted a brief which included a fake

                                  11   citation to nonexistent law, caused in part by use of AI and ultimately from a lack of care and lack

                                  12   of supervision.

                                   6                                           LEGAL STANDARD

                                   7          In language familiar to lawyers appearing in federal courts, Rule 11(b) states that “[b]y

                                   8   presenting to the court a pleading, written motion, or other paper—whether by signing, filing,

                                   9   submitting, or later advocating it—an attorney or unrepresented party certifies that to the best of the

                                  10   person's knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the

                                  11   circumstances . . . the claims, defenses, and other legal contentions are warranted by existing law or

                                  12   by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law or for establishing
Northern District of California
 United States District Court




                                  13   new law[.]” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(2).

                                  14          A lawyer whose name appears on a court filing therefore “certifies to the court that the signer

                                  15   has read the document, has conducted a reasonable inquiry into the facts and the law and is satisfied

                                  16   that the document is well grounded in both, and is acting without any improper motive.” Bus.

                                  17   Guides, Inc. v. Chromatic Commc'ns. Enters., Inc., 498 U.S. 533, 542 (1991). Accordingly, Rule

                                  18   11 “admits of no exceptions to the requirement that all reasonable attorneys will read a document

                                  19   before filing it in court.” Zaldivar v. City of Los Angeles, 780 F.2d 823, 830 (9th Cir. 1986),

                                  20   abrogated on other grounds by Cooter & Gell v. Hartmarx Corp., 496 U.S. 384 (1990). Thus, the

                                  21   lawyer whose name appears on a court filing has “an affirmative duty to conduct a reasonable

                                  22   inquiry into the facts and the law before filing.” Bus. Guides, 498 U.S. at 551 (emphasis added).

                                  23   “[T]he applicable standard is one of reasonableness under the circumstances.” Id. “Courts must

                                  24   apply an objective test in assessing whether the rule has been violated.” Yagman v. Republic Ins.,

                                  25   987 F.2d 622, 628 (9th Cir. 1993). Therefore, a “violation of the rule does not require subjective

                                  26   bad faith.” Id.

                                  27          The Court has authority under Rule 11(c) to impose sanctions for violations of the Rule. See

                                  28   Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c). Rule 11 assumes a signer read the document, because “obligations imposed
                                                                                         4
                                       Case 3:23-cv-06558-PHK            Document 230         Filed 04/28/26     Page 5 of 17




                                   1   under subdivision (b) obviously require that a pleading, written motion, or other paper be read before

                                   2   it is filed or submitted to the court.” Adv. Comm’ee Notes to 1993 Amendment to Fed. R. Civ. P.

                                   3   11(a).

                                   4            Long before the public launch of various AI or large language models such as ChatGPT, the

                                   5   Fifth Circuit affirmed an award of sanctions by noting that “[f]iling mountainous piles of

                                   6   unorganized documents and citing to nonexistent rules of law are precisely the sort of conduct that,

                                   7   under the objective test of Rule 11, could lead a district court to conclude that the attorney had not

                                   8   made reasonable inquiry into the law or was seeking to harass or delay.” Willy, 855 F.2d at 1172.

                                   9   Sanctioning a party for citing to nonexistent law is, unfortunately, not merely a product of AI-

                                  10   hallucinated citations. See, e.g., Dobson v. U.S. Bank Trust, N.A., 2021 WL 1235248 at *2 (C.D.

                                  11   Cal. Feb. 19, 2021) (“Plaintiff's citation to a nonexistent code section and subsequent failure to

                                  12   address the erroneous citation and the merits of Defendant's Motion for Sanctions indicate that
Northern District of California
 United States District Court




                                  13   Plaintiff did not conduct a reasonable and competent inquiry into the law before signing and filing

                                  14   his Complaint.”). “One specific inquiry for determining whether sanctions are appropriate is

                                  15   ‘whether the party or attorney made a reasonable inquiry into the law.’” Mid Central Operating

                                  16   Engineers Health and Welfare Fund v. HoosierVac LLC, 2025 WL 574234 at *2 (S.D. Ind. Feb. 21,

                                  17   2025) (quoting Brown v. Federation of State Med. Boards of the U.S., 830 F.2d 1429, 1435 (7th Cir.

                                  18   1987), overruled on other grounds by Mars Steel Corp. v. Continental Bank N.A., 880 F.2d 928 (7th

                                  19   Cir. 1989)). The duty to check every paper filed in court for accuracy and veracity is nondelegable

                                  20   to other attorneys or to technology. See, e.g., Unioil, Inc. v. E.F. Hutton & Co., 809 F.2d 548, 558

                                  21   (9th Cir. 1986), overruled on other grounds by In re Keegan Mgmt. Co., Sec. Litig., 78 F.3d 431,

                                  22   434–35 (9th Cir. 1996) (“An attorney who signs the pleading cannot simply delegate to forwarding

                                  23   co-counsel his duty of reasonable inquiry.”); Versant Funding LLC v. Teras Breakbulk Ocean

                                  24   Navigation Enters., LLC, 2025 WL 1440351, at *4–5 (S.D. Fla. May 20, 2025).

                                  25            “Given the rise of artificial intelligence and its use in court proceedings, courts have begun

                                  26   ordering litigants suspected of citing to AI-hallucinated cases to show cause why sanctions or

                                  27   discipline should not issue. Specifically, courts tend to find that Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b)(2) applies in

                                  28   the context of litigants citing to AI-hallucinated cases in documents submitted to courts.” Marion

                                  19   Webb attempted to minimize the impact of the false citation in the record, calling it a “single

                                  20   occurrence and isolated event of a ‘Frankensteinian legal citation’” and a “single and isolated event.”

                                  21   [Dkt. 178 at ⁋⁋ 9, 19]. This argument misses the point: if an attorney engages in multiple instances

                                  22   of submitting fake or hallucinated citations to a court, that may be evidence of pervasive misconduct

                                  23   (or a pattern and practice of misconduct), but that does not logically lead to the conclusion that a

                                  24   single instance of submitting a fake citation is somehow exempt from sanctions. Many bad acts are

                                  25   worse than a single bad act, but that does not mean that a single bad act is somehow irreproachable.

                                  26   “Even a single fabricated citation would be serious. Indeed, other courts have imposed Rule 11

                                  27   sanctions in such an instance. This Court is not minimizing the use of a single fake case.” Mavy v.

                                  28   Comm’r of Soc. Sec. Admin., 2025 WL 2355222 at *8 n.5 (D. Ariz. Aug. 14, 2025) (citation
