10                                III.       LEGAL STANDARD
11          A court “shall grant summary judgment if the movant shows that there is no
12   genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a
13   matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). A disputed fact is “material” where it might
14   affect the outcome of the suit under the governing law, and the dispute is “genuine”
15   where “the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the
16   nonmoving party.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). The
17   burden of establishing the absence of a genuine issue of material fact lies with the
18   moving party. See Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322–23 (1986).
19          Once the moving party satisfies its initial burden, the nonmoving party cannot
20   simply rest on the pleadings or argue that any disagreement or “metaphysical doubt”
21   about a material issue of fact precludes summary judgment. Matsushita Elec. Indus.
22   v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 586 (1986); see Celotex, 477 U.S. at 324. The
23   non-moving party must show that there are “genuine factual issues that . . . may
24   reasonably be resolved in favor of either party.” Cal. Architectural Bldg. Prods., Inc.

 3   a party who fails to make a sufficient showing on an element essential to her case
 4   when she will ultimately bear the burden of proof at trial. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322–

 6         In ruling on summary judgment motions, courts “view the facts and draw
 7   reasonable inferences in the light most favorable” to the nonmoving party. Scott v.
 8   Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 378 (2007) (internal quotation marks omitted). Thus, when
 9   parties file cross-motions for summary judgment, the court “evaluate[s] each motion
10   separately, giving the nonmoving party in each instance the benefit of all reasonable
11   inferences.” A.C.L.U. of Nev. v. City of Las Vegas, 466 F.3d 784, 790–91 (9th Cir.
12   2006). The court considers “each party’s evidence, regardless under which motion the
13   evidence is offered.” Las Vegas Sands, LLC v. Nehme, 632 F.3d 526, 532 (9th Cir.
14   2011). Conclusory, speculative, or “uncorroborated and self-serving” testimony will
15   not raise genuine issues of fact sufficient to defeat summary judgment. Villiarimo v.
16   Aloha Island Air, Inc., 281 F.3d 1054, 1061 (9th Cir. 2002); Thornhill Publ’g Co. v.
17   GTE Tel. & Elec. Corp., 594 F.2d 730, 738 (9th Cir. 1979). Moreover, though the
18   Court may not weigh conflicting evidence or make credibility determinations, there
19   must be more than a mere scintilla of contradictory evidence to survive summary
20   judgment. Addisu v. Fred Meyer, Inc., 198 F.3d 1130, 1134 (9th Cir. 2000).
21         The Court may assume that material facts claimed and adequately supported are
22   undisputed except to the extent that such material facts are (a) included in the
23   opposing party’s responsive statement of disputes and (b) controverted by declaration
24   or competent written evidence. C.D. Cal. L.R. 56-4. The Court is not obligated to
25   look any further in the record for supporting evidence other than what is actually and
26   specifically referenced. Id.

 1                                IV.       DISCUSSION
 2         Transdev and Ogilvie each move for summary judgment on all of Chaney’s
 3   claims. (See generally OMSJ; TMSJ.) Specifically, Ogilvie argues that Chaney does
 4   not have prudential standing to bring her harassment claim against him because the
 5   claim belongs to the bankruptcy trustee appointed to Chaney’s estate. (OMSJ 10–11.)
 6   In its motion, Transdev adopts Ogilvie’s analysis and argues that it also applies to all
 7   of Chaney’s claims against Transdev. (TMSJ 8.)
 8         “Under the Bankruptcy Code, ‘all legal or equitable interests of the debtor in
 9   property as of the commencement of a bankruptcy case’ belong to the bankruptcy
10   estate created at the debtor’s filing of a bankruptcy petition.”      Bercy v. City of
11   Phoenix, 103 F.4th 591, 594 (9th Cir. 2024). Property of the estate includes “all legal
12   or equitable interests of the debtor in property as of the commencement of the case,”
13   including “the debtor’s ‘causes of action.’” Smith v. Arthur Andersen LLP, 421 F.3d
14   989, 1002 (9th Cir. 2005) (first citing 11 U.S.C. § 541(a)(1); and then citing United
15   States v. Whiting Pools, Inc., 462 U.S. 198, 205 n.9 (1983)). Once a bankruptcy estate
16   is created, “only the bankruptcy trustee,” who is the “representative of the estate and
17   the real party in interest in a suit on the estate’s claims, has capacity to sue on the
18   estate’s behalf.” Bercy, 103 F.4th at 594–95 (citation modified) (citing 11 U.S.C.
19   § 323(a)–(b)). Debtors lack prudential standing to do so. Id.
20         The Court starts from a position that the parties do not appear to dispute: as of
21   June 21, 2023, the date that Chaney filed bankruptcy, (see Bankr. Dkt.), Chaney’s
22   claims belonged to her estate. There can be no dispute because Chaney’s claims in
23   this action stem from conduct that allegedly took place between 2019 and 2020. (See
24   TSUF ¶¶ 5, 133.) As the claims are based on conduct that occurred before her
25   bankruptcy petition, which Chaney filed in 2023, the claims “belong to the bankruptcy
26   estate.” Bercy, 103 F.4th at 595. Thus, as of the date Chaney filed bankruptcy, only
27   the bankruptcy trustee had standing to bring the underlying claims.

 1         The crux of the parties’ dispute is whether the bankruptcy trustee then
 2   abandoned Chaney’s claims, allowing her to bring this suit. (Opp’n OMSJ 16–17,
 3   Dkt. No. 36; Reply OMSJ 9–10, Dkt. No. 37.) Under 11 U.S.C. § 554(a), “[a]fter
 4   notice and a hearing, the trustee may abandon any property of the estate that is
 5   burdensome to the estate or that is of inconsequential value and benefit to the estate.”
 6   Courts may also consider property abandoned to the debtor where the property is
 7   “scheduled . . . [and] not otherwise administered at the time of the closing of a
 8   [bankruptcy case].” 11 U.S.C. § 554(c) (emphasis added). If a bankruptcy trustee
 9   abandons a particular claim, “the abandonment could constitute the estate’s
10   ratification of [the debtor’s] lawsuit,” allowing the debtor to bring the lawsuit “as if
11   the estate itself had originally commenced the action.” Dunmore v. United States,
12   358 F.3d 1107, 1112 (9th Cir. 2004).
13         Here however, the documentary evidence demonstrates that the trustee did not
14   abandon Chaney’s claims.      First, there is no evidence that the trustee expressly
15   abandoned any property after “notice and a hearing” pursuant to 11 U.S.C. § 554(a).
16   It is undisputed that, when the bankruptcy trustee recommended discharge on
17   August 8, 2023, he expressly declared that he found no “property belonging to the
18   estate” and “no property available for distribution from the estate.” (Bankr. Dkt. 4.)
19   It is also undisputed that Chaney had knowledge of the claims at issue here but failed
20   to schedule them for the trustee to evaluate.         (See TSUF ¶¶ 5, 133, 141–43
21   (establishing Chaney’s knowledge of her employment dispute claims); Bankr. Pet.)
22   From these undisputed facts, the only reasonable inference the Court can draw is that
23   the bankruptcy trustee did not know of Chaney’s pending claims when he
24   recommended discharge.
25         Chaney attempts to raise a factual dispute by asserting that the “trustee had
26   notice of pending litigation yet certified zero assets, demonstrating his determination
27   that disclosed claims had no value to the estate or were abandoned.”            (Opp’n
28   OMSJ 17.) However, as explained, this assertion runs contrary to the documentary




                                                6
Case 2:24-cv-10761-ODW-AJR        Document 46 Filed 04/28/26        Page 7 of 12 Page ID

 1   evidence. The trustee recommended discharge prior to receiving notice of Chaney’s
 2   pending claims. (See Bankr. Dkt.) Chaney only amended her petition to add her
 3   pending claims after the bankruptcy trustee recommended discharge. (See id.) Thus,
 4   the bankruptcy trustee could not have had notice of Chaney’s claims, and therefore,
 5   could not have abandoned them, prior to the Bankruptcy Court’s discharge.
 6   Moreover, Chaney fails to support her assertion with any citation to evidence that
 7   could create a material factual dispute. Thus, the Court disregards this purported
 8   factual dispute as unsupported. See Keenan v. Allan, 91 F.3d 1275, 1279 (“[I]t is not
 9   our task . . . to scour the record in search of a genuine issue of triable fact. We rely on
10   the nonmoving party to identify with reasonable particularity the evidence that
11   precludes summary judgment.”). As the bankruptcy trustee did not know of Chaney’s
12   pending claims, he could not have expressly abandoned them after notice and a
13   hearing under 11 U.S.C. § 554(a).
14          Second, Chaney did not “schedule” her claims in her initial bankruptcy petition,
15   meaning that her claims could not have been later abandoned by the bankruptcy
16   trustee under 11 U.S.C. § 554(c). In her initial bankruptcy petition, Chaney failed to
17   disclose her claims despite clear language asking whether she had any “[c]laims
18   against third parties,” including “employment disputes.” (Bankr. Pet. 18.) Chaney’s
19   failure to schedule her claims “on her bankruptcy petition” means that “she is divested
20   of prudential standing to assert that cause of action.” Neal v. NaturalCare, Inc.,
21   No. 5:12-cv-00531-DOC (OPx), 2014 WL 346639, at *3 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 30, 2014)
22   (citing Dunmore, 358 F.3d at 1112). Indeed, 11 U.S.C. § 554(c) is clear that courts
23   may consider only scheduled property as abandoned to the debtor. As Chaney failed
24   to properly schedule her claims prior to the trustee’s discharge recommendation, the
25   Court cannot consider her claims to be abandoned by the bankruptcy trustee. Thus,
26   the bankruptcy trustee, and not Chaney, continues to have standing to bring the claims
27   in this action.

 8           Two overarching factors drive the Court’s sanctions order. First, the imposition
 9   of sanctions for submitting generative-AI hallucinations is so well-documented at this
10   point that the Court finds the failure to verify citations after using generative-AI rises
