 4                                   III.   LEGAL STANDARDS

 5           Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11 “imposes a duty on attorneys to certify that they have

 6   conducted a reasonable inquiry and have determined that any papers filed with the court are well

 7   grounded in fact, legally tenable, and not interposed for any improper purpose.” Cooter & Gell v.

 8   Hartmarx Corp., 496 U.S. 384, 393 (1990) (internal quotation marks omitted). Rule 11 provides,

 9   in relevant part, that:

10           By presenting to the court a pleading, written motion, or other paper—whether by
             signing, filing, submitting, or later advocating it—an attorney or unrepresented
11           party certifies that to the best of the person’s knowledge, information, and belief,
             formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances:
12
             ...
13
             (2) the claims, defenses, and other legal contentions are warranted by existing law
14           or by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law
             or for establishing new law.
15
     Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(b). “On its own, the court may order an attorney, law firm, or party to show
16
     cause why conduct specifically described in the order has not violated Rule 11(b).” Fed. R. Civ.
17
     P. 11(c)(3).
18
             Courts have “wide discretion in determining the appropriate sanction for a Rule 11
19
     violation.” Hudson v. Moore Bus. Forms, Inc., 836 F.2d 1156, 1163 (9th Cir. 1987). “A sanction
20
     imposed under this rule must be limited to what suffices to deter repetition of the conduct or
21
     comparable conduct by others similarly situated.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(4). Additionally, sua
22
     sponte sanctions under Rule 11 “will ordinarily be imposed only in situations that are akin to a
23
     contempt of court.” United Nat’l Ins. Co. v. R&D Latex Corp., 242 F.3d 1102, 1116 (9th Cir.
24


     ORDER ON SANCTIONS - 4
           Case 3:24-cv-05808-TMC            Document 265        Filed 07/24/26       Page 5 of 11




 1   2001) (emphasis omitted) (quoting Barber v. Miller, 146 F.3d 707, 711 (9th Cir. 1998)). “[I]n the

 2   Ninth Circuit, the ‘akin to a contempt’ standard ‘generally requires the court to find bad faith or

 3   conduct tantamount to bad faith.’” Mavy v. Comm’r of Soc. Sec. Admin., No. CV-25-00689-

 4   PHX-KML (ASB), 2026 WL 91483, at *6 (D. Ariz. Jan. 13, 2026) (quoting Rocha v. Fiedler,

 5   No. 24-3692, 2025 WL 1219007, at *1 (9th Cir. Apr. 28, 2025)).

 6                                        IV.     DISCUSSION

 7          Ms. Stewart concedes that she violated Rule 11 by failing to properly review her

 8   submissions to this Court, resulting in dozens of false citations. Dkt. 245 at 28, 33. The Court

 9   must therefore determine (1) whether her conduct was akin to contempt and (2) if so, what

10   sanctions are appropriate.

11   A.     Ms. Stewart’s conduct was akin to contempt, and the Court may issue sanctions sua
            sponte.
12
            Ms. Stewart’s conduct was tantamount to bad faith and therefore akin to contempt. Her
13
     false citations “constitute fundamental, material, legal and factual errors submitted to the Court
14
     because AI-generated work product . . . was subjected to no discernable scrutiny.” Parker v.
15
     Costco Wholesale Corp., No. C25-0519-SKV, 2025 WL 4228413, at *11 (W.D. Wash. Nov. 7,
16
     2025). “That failure was compounded by [Ms. Stewart] certifying, under Rule 11, that [s]he had
17
     conducted [a] reasonable inquiry into the supportability of h[er] filings knowing full well [s]he
18
     had not done so.” Id.
19
            Ms. Stewart complied with the Court’s order to show cause—admitting that she
20
     generated the false citations, accepting full responsibility for her conduct, and noting that she had
21
     ceased using artificial intelligence “to generate quotations or citations for filings submitted to
22
     any court.” Dkt. 245 at 28–30, 33. While the Court appreciates Ms. Stewart’s responsiveness,
23
     several factors weigh toward a finding of bad faith.

 1           First, the volume and severity of Ms. Stewart’s violations show that her submissions were

 2   not “subjected to any meaningful scrutiny.” Parker, 2025 WL 4228413, at *8. “Many of the

 3   errors were obvious on the face of the document. A competent attorney would have, upon review

 4   of the arguments and authority cited, flagged that something was wrong.” Id. But Ms. Stewart

 5   admits that she simply did not review the outputs of ChatGPT and Claude because she believed

 6   them to be without error. See Dkt. 245 at 7 (“Using automation in this manner gave me

 7   confidence that . . . so long as the inputs were reliable, the output would be accurate.”); id. at 15

 8   (“I obtained that characterization from the tool when I asked it to summarize what Dr. Kelman

 9   relied upon, and I did not verify it against his report before including it in the motion.”); id. at 17

10   (“I did not verify the accuracy of the citation information that the tool generated against the

11   actual articles.”).

12           Ms. Stewart’s blind trust in these programs was unfounded. “[A] lawyer using technology

13   in law practice is obliged to do so competently.” Wash. State Bar Ass’n, Advisory Opinion

14   202505 (Nov. 20, 2025). “[T]he imposition of sanctions for submitting generative-AI

15   hallucinations is so well-documented at this point” that Ms. Stewart’s repeated failure to verify

16   citations generated by these programs “rises to the level of bad faith.” Chaney v. Transdev Servs.

17   Inc., No. 2:24-CV-10761-ODW (AJRX), 2026 WL 1146736, at *6 (C.D. Cal. Apr. 28, 2026).

18   Ms. Stewart even states that she was initially skeptical about using generative artificial

19   intelligence because she had previously heard of an attorney being sanctioned for “us[ing] what

20   was generated without checking it.” Dkt. 245 at 2. And by the time Ms. Stewart filed the

21   offending motions in late 2025, numerous Ninth Circuit courts had issued or threatened sanctions

22   for similar misconduct. See, e.g., Twist It Up, Inc. v. Annie Int’l, Inc., No. SA CV 24-736-DMG

23   (ADSX), 2025 WL 3049841, at *2 (C.D. Cal. June 26, 2025); Ligeri v. Amazon.com Servs. LLC,

 3          Ms. Stewart avers that she believed the programs she used would generate correct

12   bad faith may be inferred.” Ramirez v. Humala, No. 24-CV-242 (RPK) (JAM), 2025 WL

13   1384161, at *2 (E.D.N.Y. May 13, 2025) (quoting In re 60 E. 80th St. Equities, Inc., 218 F.3d

14   109, 115 (2d Cir. 2000)).

15          Second, the citation errors in the expert evidence are especially flagrant because they

16   indicate Ms. Stewart “may have been so involved with the drafting of expert reports as to

17   ‘jeopardize the experts’ independence.’” Dkt. 209 at 5 (quoting Gerke v. Travelers Cas. Ins. Co.

18   of Am., 289 F.R.D. 316, 326 (D. Or. 2013)); see also McClellan v. I-Flow Corp., 710 F. Supp. 2d

19   1092, 1118 (D. Or. 2010) (“[E]xpert reports may be discredited if they ‘merely express the

20   opinions of the lawyers who hired them.’” (quoting Trigon Ins. Co. v. United States, 204 F.R.D.

21   277, 294 (E.D. Va. 2001))). Ms. Stewart now states that she used Claude or ChatGPT to

22   “generate a formatted citation table” for academic articles and “provided the same AI-generated

23   citation table to both experts as an appendix, and neither expert caught the errors in the citation

24   data before signing their reports.” Dkt. 245 at 17–18. Although the Court has already struck these

 3           Third, Defendants first raised their concerns about AI hallucinations during oral argument

 4   on December 2, 2025, when they provided the Court and Ms. Stewart with a table of allegedly

 5   false citations in Plaintiff’s Daubert motion. Dkt. 187 at 48. On December 9, Defendants filed a

 6   similar table with respect to Plaintiff’s motion to strike affirmative defenses. Dkt. 191-1. Several

 7   citations in these tables were clearly erroneous and ultimately formed the basis for the Court’s

 8   order to show cause and the present order. Dkt. 209 at 3–4. Ms. Stewart should have reviewed

 9   these citations with urgency and—upon realizing that they were erroneous—revisited all

10   citations that she generated with artificial intelligence, withdrawn or amended all motions

11   containing those citations, and explained her mistakes to the Court. See, e.g., Mavy, 2026 WL

14   submitted a proposed brief containing correct citations[, and] proactively investigated the

15   contract attorney’s work in different cases and found ‘four other briefs containing erroneous

16   citations’”).

 8   B.      Sanctions

 9           The Court must now determine what types of sanctions against Ms. Stewart are sufficient

10   to deter future, similar misconduct from her and other attorneys. “Rule 11 sanctions are meant to

11   serve several purposes, including (1) deterring future litigation abuse, (2) punishing present

12   litigation abuse, (3) compensating victims of litigation abuse, and (4) streamlining court dockets

13   and facilitating case management.” Wadsworth v. Walmart Inc., 348 F.R.D. 489, 496–97

14   (D. Wyo. 2025) (quoting White v. Gen. Motors Corp., 908 F.2d 675, 683 (10th Cir. 1990)).

15           Sanctions for fabricated citations may include “(1) monetary sanctions, (2) striking the

16   filing containing erroneous citations, (3) requiring written notification to the client, (4) requiring

17   written notification to judges incorrectly identified as having authored nonexistent cases, and

18   (5) referring attorneys to the appropriate disciplinary body for disciplinary proceedings.” Oneto,

19   808 F. Supp. 3d at 980 (collecting cases); see, e.g., Chaney, 2026 WL 1146736, at *6 (imposing

20   a $2,500 fine and requiring that the attorney file a declaration explaining the court’s findings of

21   bad faith in his other cases in the district); Tercero v. Sacramento Logistics, LLC, No. 2:24-CV-

22   00953-DC-JDP, 2025 WL 2605020, at *13 (E.D. Cal. Sept. 9, 2025) (imposing a $1,500 fine,

23   requiring that the attorney serve a copy of the court’s sanctions order on her client, and directing

24   the clerk of court to serve the order on the California Bar).

 1          “[A]ny sanction imposed must be proportionate to the offense and commensurate with

 2   principles of restraint and dignity inherent in judicial power.” Oneto, 808 F. Supp. 3d at 980

 3   (quoting Zambrano v. City of Tustin, 885 F.2d 1473, 1480 (9th Cir. 1989)). Courts handling

 4   similar cases tend toward leniency where attorneys are forthcoming about their misuse of

 5   artificial intelligence and take significant steps to correct their mistakes. See, e.g., Dodge v.

 6   FirstService Residential Ariz. LLC, No. CV-24-01550-PHX-SMM, 2025 WL 3653164, at *3

 7   (D. Ariz. Dec. 17, 2025) (imposing no sanctions where an attorney “openly and honestly

 8   admitted to his improper use of AI, including the submission of hallucinated citations” and “took

 9   the initiative to self-report to the State Bar for his improper use of AI and contend[ed] that he

10   [was] reviewing all other pending matters”).

11          Here, Ms. Stewart’s actions warrant significant sanctions. Ms. Stewart submitted dozens

12   of false citations throughout four briefs and two expert reports. When Defendants identified

13   multiple clearly erroneous citations in December 2025, Ms. Stewart was not proactive in

14   rectifying her mistakes. Her attempt to correct the citations in her Daubert motion was

15   incomplete and confusing, and she did not explain how the errors occurred. After making those

16   corrections, she then submitted a brief containing one additional hallucinated citation.

17          The Court acknowledges that Ms. Stewart responded to the order to show cause by fully

18   admitting fault and explaining how her unverified use of artificial intelligence caused each error.

19   At the same time, “submitting fake citations and filing unverified generative AI outputs . . .

20   prejudices clients, undermines faith in attorneys and the justice system, and compels diversion of

21   public resources from pending cases.” Parker, 2025 WL 4228413, at *9. The Court spent

22   significant time reviewing and addressing Ms. Stewart’s false citations while managing other

23   matters on its docket, including a high volume of time-sensitive immigration habeas matters.
