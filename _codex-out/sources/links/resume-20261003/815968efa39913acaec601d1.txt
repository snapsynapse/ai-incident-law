14            Although the Court appreciates Ms. Tate’s honesty and expressions of contrition in
15   response to the OSC and agrees that the conduct at issue here is not as egregious as some
16   of the conduct that has warranted severe sanctions in other cases, the Court still concludes
17   that some form of sanction is warranted. As discussed in more detail in the July 8, 2026
18   order,    potential   sanctions    in   this   context    include     “disbarment,    suspension,
19   reprimand/censure (‘public discipline which declares the conduct of the lawyer improper,
20   but does not limit the lawyer’s right to practice’), admonition (‘private reprimand’), and
21   probation,” and the factors to consider when deciding which sanction to impose include
22   “(a) the duty violated; (b) the lawyer’s mental state; (c) the actual or potential injury caused
23   by the lawyer’s misconduct; and (d) the existence of aggravating or mitigating factors.”
24   (Id. at 10, cleaned up.) Here, Ms. Tate violated multiple duties; acted with a mental state
25   that, if not intentional, was at least negligent and was arguably reckless; and created at least
26   a potential for injury—had the Court uncritically accepted her fake quotations and
27   inaccurate case citation, this could have led to legal errors that may have derailed the
28   trajectory of the case. Finally, although Ms. Tate’s responses to the OSC establish that this

 4             When it comes to “discipline for misrepresentations to a court,” the Ninth Circuit
 5   employs the ABA Standards, which depend “in large measure, on the lawyer’s mental
 6   state”:
 7             6.12. Suspension is generally appropriate when a lawyer knows that false
               statements or documents are being submitted to the court or that material
 8             information is improperly being withheld, and takes no remedial action, and
               causes injury or potential injury to a party to the legal proceeding, or causes
 9             an adverse or potentially adverse effect on the legal proceeding.
10             6.13. Reprimand is generally appropriate when a lawyer is negligent either
               in determining whether statements or documents are false or in taking
11             remedial action when material information is being withheld and causes
               injury or potential injury to a party to the legal proceeding, or causes an
12             adverse or potentially adverse effect on the legal proceeding.
13             6.14. Admonition is generally appropriate when a lawyer engages in an
               isolated instance of neglect in determining whether submitted statements or
14             documents are false or in failing to disclose material information upon
               learning of its falsity, and causes little or no actual or potential injury to a
15             party, or causes little or no adverse or potentially adverse effect on the legal
               proceeding.
16
17   In re Girardi, 611 F.3d 1027, 1038 (9th Cir. 2010).
18             As discussed above, this case involved conduct that was at least negligent (albeit
19   not knowingly deceptive), was not isolated, created a potential for injury, and was
20   accompanied by several aggravating factors. Under these circumstances, no sanction less
21   severe than a reprimand would be appropriate.
