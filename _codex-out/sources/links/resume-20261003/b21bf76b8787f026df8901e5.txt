       On September 2, 2026, counsel for Brigham Young University (“BYU”), Chad Pehrson

and Robert S. Clark (“Counsel”), appeared before the Court upon an Order to Show Cause1 after

BYU filed briefing containing errors and citations that appeared to be AI-hallucinated.2

       Prior to the issuance of the Order to Show Cause, Mr. Pehrson filed a Notice of Errata

identifying and correcting twelve errors embodied in the briefing.3 Prior to the hearing, Counsel

filed a Notice of Acknowledgment wherein Counsel apologized for the harm caused by the errors

and stated they had refunded opposing counsels’ legal fees incurred as a result.4 At the hearing,

Mr. Pehrson stated he drafted the briefing, which included a citation to a nonexistent case and

several citations to cases that do not support and are unrelated to the stated propositions, some of

which misstated the controlling law of the dispute. Mr. Pehrson also stated that he relied on

several AI models in drafting the briefing, including ClearBrief, Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini.

Mr. Pehrson explained that the errors resulted from his failure to review the accuracy of the legal

contentions contained therein. Mr. Clark also explained his failure to perform a review of the

filings prior to submission, and he informed the Court that he enrolled in a CLE course relating

to the use of AI in the legal profession.

       The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provide that presenting a legal document to the

court, “whether by signing, filing, submitting, or later advocating it,” certifies that the attorneys

have conducted a reasonable inquiry to ensure the legal contentions therein are “warranted by

existing law,” or by a nonfrivolous argument to extend, modify, or reverse it, or establish new

law.5 Based on Counsels’ admissions at the show cause hearing, the Court finds that Mr. Pehrson

and Mr. Clark violated Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(b) by failing to conduct a reasonable

inquiry into the existence and accuracy of the legal authority cited in the briefing submitted to

the Court that was riddled with AI-generated errors.

       The Court takes misuse of AI, and the harm flowing therefrom, very seriously. As one

court has stated:

       The Court’s time is taken from other important endeavors. The client may be
       deprived of arguments based on authentic judicial precedents. There is potential
       harm to the reputation of judges and courts whose names are falsely invoked as
       authors of the bogus opinions and to the reputation of a party attributed with
       fictional conduct. It promotes cynicism about the legal profession and the American
       judicial system. And a future litigant may be tempted to defy a judicial ruling by
       disingenuously claiming doubt about its authenticity.6
