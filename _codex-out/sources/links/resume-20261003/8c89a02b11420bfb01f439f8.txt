 1                                   III.   LEGAL STANDARDS

 2   A.     Summary Judgment

            “The court shall grant summary judgment if the movant shows that there is no genuine
 3
     dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R.
 4
     Civ. P. 56(a). A dispute as to a material fact is genuine “if the evidence is such that a reasonable
 5
     jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party.” Villiarimo v. Aloha Island Air, Inc., 281
 6
     F.3d 1054, 1061 (9th Cir. 2002) (quoting Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248
 7
     (1986)). The moving party has the initial burden of “‘showing’—that is, pointing out to the
 8
     district court—that there is an absence of evidence to support the nonmoving party’s case.”
 9
     Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 325 (1986). If the moving party meets its initial burden,
10
     the non-moving party must go beyond the pleadings and “set forth specific facts showing that
11
     there is a genuine issue for trial.” Zellmer v. Meta Platforms, Inc., 104 F.4th 1117, 1122 (9th Cir.
12
     2024) (quoting Anderson, 477 U.S. at 256).
13
            The evidence relied upon by either party must be able to be “presented in a form that
14
     would be admissible in evidence.” See Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(2). “An affidavit or declaration used
15
     to support or oppose a motion must be made on personal knowledge, set out facts that would be
16
     admissible in evidence, and show that the affiant or declarant is competent to testify on the
17
     matters stated.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(4); see also Fed. R. Evid. 602 (“A witness may testify to a
18
     matter only if evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal
19
     knowledge of the matter. Evidence to prove personal knowledge may consist of the witness’s
20
     own testimony.”).
21
            Conclusory, nonspecific statements in affidavits are not sufficient, and “missing facts”
22
     will not be “presume[d].” Lujan v. Nat’l Wildlife Fed’n, 497 U.S. 871, 889 (1990). However,
23
     “[t]he evidence of the nonmovant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn
24

 1   in his favor.” Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 651 (2014) (per curiam) (alteration in original)

 2   (quoting Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255). Consequently, “a District Court must resolve any factual

 3   issues of controversy in favor of the non-moving party only in the sense that, where the facts

 4   specifically averred by that party contradict facts specifically averred by the movant, the motion

 5   must be denied.” Lujan, 497 U.S. at 888 (internal quotations omitted).

 6   B.        Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and Daubert

 7             Federal Rule of Evidence 702 governs the admissibility of expert testimony. “Expert

 8   testimony must be both relevant and reliable.” United States v. Vallejo, 237 F.3d 1008, 1019 (9th

 9   Cir. 2001). Before trial, “[t]he trial court acts as a ‘gatekeeper’ to exclude expert testimony that”

10   does not meet these standards. Neal-Lomax v. Las Vegas Metro. Police Dep’t, 574 F. Supp. 2d

11   1193, 1201 (D. Nev. 2008) (quoting Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137, 145 (1999)).

12             Expert testimony is relevant if it will help “the trier of fact to understand the evidence or

13   to determine a fact in issue.” Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc. (Daubert I), 509 U.S. 579,

14   591 (1993) (quoting Fed. R. Evid. 702(a)); see also Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.

15   (Daubert II), 43 F.3d 1311, 1315 (9th Cir. 1995) (explaining that relevant evidence is that which

16   “logically advances a material aspect of the proposing party’s case”), cert. denied, 516 U.S. 869

17   (1995).

18             Expert testimony is reliable if it “is based on sufficient facts or data,” “is the product of

19   reliable principles and methods,” and “reflects a reliable application of the principles and

20   methods to the facts of the case.” Fed. R. Evid. 702(b), (c), (d). More generally, evidence is

21   reliable if it “has a reliable basis in the knowledge and experience of [the relevant] discipline.”

22   United States v. Sandoval-Mendoza, 472 F.3d 645, 654 (9th Cir. 2006) (alteration in original)

23   (quoting Kumho Tire, 526 U.S. at 149). In Daubert, the Supreme Court set out several factors

24   that courts consider in determining reliability:


     ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT - 5
           Case 3:24-cv-05808-TMC             Document 269          Filed 08/18/26       Page 6 of 19




 1           (1) whether a scientific theory or technique can be (and has been) tested;
             (2) whether the theory or technique has been subjected to peer review and
 2           publication; (3) the known or potential rate of error and the existence and
             maintenance of standards controlling the techniques operation; and (4) whether the
 3           technique is generally accepted.

 4   Neal-Lomax, 574 F. Supp. 2d at 1201 (quoting Daubert I, 509 U.S. at 593–94). But these factors

 5   are not a “definitive checklist or test,” see Daubert I, 509 U.S. at 593, and “[i]n other cases, the

 6   relevant reliability concerns may focus upon personal knowledge or experience.” Kumho Tire,

 7   526 U.S. at 150. Nor do the Daubert factors “necessarily apply even in every instance in which

 8   the reliability of scientific testimony is challenged.” Id. at 151. Rather, “Rule 702 grants the

 9   district judge the discretionary authority . . . to determine reliability in light of the particular facts

10   and circumstances of the particular case.” Id. at 158; see also Hangarter v. Provident Life &

11   Accident Ins. Co., 373 F.3d 998, 1017 (9th Cir. 2004) (“[A] trial court not only has broad latitude

12   in determining whether an expert’s testimony is reliable, but also in deciding how to determine

13   the testimony’s reliability.” (internal quotation marks and emphasis omitted)).

15   A.      The Court GRANTS Defendants’ motion to exclude Ms. Evelyn Cadman (Dkt. 206).

16           Defendants ask the Court to exclude expert evidence from Ms. Evelyn Cadman, retained

17   by Plaintiff to render “opinions regarding the regulatory status of the dietary supplement

18   product” sold to Plaintiff by Defendants. Dkt. 206-1 at 2. According to Defendants, Cadman

19   impermissibly “offers only legal advocacy—applying federal statutes and regulations to alleged

20   facts and pronouncing whether Defendants violated the law—telling the Court and jury what

21   judgments to reach.” Dkt. 206 at 1. The Court agrees with Defendants.

22           While Federal Rule of Evidence 704(a) states that “[a]n opinion is not objectionable just

23   because it embraces an ultimate issue,” the Ninth Circuit “has repeatedly affirmed that ‘an expert

24   witness cannot give an opinion as to her legal conclusion, i.e., an opinion on an ultimate issue of


     ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT - 6
           Case 3:24-cv-05808-TMC             Document 269         Filed 08/18/26     Page 7 of 19




 1   law.’” United States v. Diaz, 876 F.3d 1194, 1197 (9th Cir. 2017) (quoting Hangarter, 373 F.3d

 2   at 1016). “This prohibition of opinion testimony on an ultimate issue of law recognizes that,

 3   ‘when an expert undertakes to tell the jury what result to reach, this does not aid the jury in

 4   making a decision, but rather attempts to substitute the expert’s judgment for the jury’s.’” Id.

 5   (alteration and emphasis omitted) (quoting United States v. Duncan, 42 F.3d 97, 101 (2d Cir.

 6   1994)).

 7             Here, Ms. Cadman’s analysis and conclusions relate solely to whether Defendants’

 8   products were “adulterated” under federal law or whether Defendants violated federal law in

 9   labeling or marketing those products. Dkt. 206-1. For example, Ms. Cadman concludes that:

10             •   [T]he product sold to [Plaintiff] was adulterated as that term is defined in 21
                   U.S.C. § 342(a)(1) and (a)(2)(C), and under the implementing regulations at 21
11                 C.F.R. Part 111 and 21 C.F.R. § 190.6. It contained one or more substances that
                   qualify as new dietary ingredients for which the required 75-day premarket
12                 notification under 21 U.S.C. § 350b and 21 C.F.R. § 190.6 was not filed.

13             •   [C]ertain ingredients in the product have pharmacological effects and are not
                   appropriately classified as dietary ingredients under 21 U.S.C. § 321(f) and 21
14                 C.F.R. §§ 101.36 and 190.10. The inclusion of such substances renders the
                   product adulterated.
15
               •   [T]he product at issue contained one or more substances that fall outside the
16                 definition of a dietary ingredient and instead meet the definition of a “drug”
                   under 21 U.S.C. § 321(g)(1), as further clarified in the regulations at 21 C.F.R.
17                 § 201.128 (intended uses) and 21 C.F.R. § 310.3 (new drugs).

18             •   [T]he product packaging and labeling for the Thesis supplement sold to
                   [Plaintiff] failed to meet the requirements of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act
19                 and associated FDA regulations concerning adequate warnings and disclosures.

20             •   [T]he Defendants’ manufacturing and quality control procedures, including
                   their handling of batch records and testing protocols, were not consistent with
21                 Current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMPs) as codified in 21 C.F.R. Part
                   111.

 1          Ms. Cadman’s opinion offers impermissible legal conclusions that are irrelevant because

 2   they do not “aid the jury in making its decision.” Fontana v. City of Auburn, No. C13-0245-JCC,

 3   2014 WL 4162528, at *6 (W.D. Wash. Aug. 21, 2014), aff’d in part, 679 F. App’x 613 (9th Cir.

 4   2017). If an expert witness uses terms that have a specialized meaning in the law and that

 5   “represent an attempt to instruct the jury on the law, or how to apply the law to the facts of the

 6   case,” the testimony is an impermissible legal conclusion. Diaz, 876 F.3d at 1198–99. Terms

 7   such as “new dietary ingredient” and “adulterated” have a “separate, distinct[,] and specialized

 8   meaning in the law different from that present in the vernacular.” Id. at 1198 (quoting United

 9   States v. McIver, 470 F.3d 550, 562 (4th Cir. 2006)). Ms. Cadman’s findings that Defendants

10   violated federal regulations are similarly impermissible legal conclusions. See Georges v.

11   Novartis Pharms. Corp., No. CV 06-5207 SJO VBKX, 2012 WL 9064768, at *9–10 (C.D. Cal.

12   Nov. 2, 2012) (allowing testimony “generally regarding pharmaceutical drugs within the context

13   of the FDA” but not “legal conclusions on . . . whether Defendant was in regulatory compliance

14   with the FDA”).

15          Citing In re Fosamax Products Liability Litigation, Plaintiff argues that Ms. Cadman may

16   opine on “general FDA regulatory requirements and procedures” and Defendants’ compliance

17   with them. Dkt. 210 at 20–21 (quoting 645 F. Supp. 2d 164, 191 (S.D.N.Y. 2009)). But

18   Ms. Cadman’s opinion is distinguishable from those of other regulatory experts that courts have

19   deemed admissible. Critically, Ms. Cadman does not offer any specialized knowledge about

20   FDA requirements that would assist a jury. Cf. In re Fosamax Prods. Liab. Litig., 645 F. Supp.

21   2d at 191 (“A lay jury cannot be expected to understand the complex regulatory framework that

22   informs the standard of care in the pharmaceutical industry. Dr. Parisian’s assessment of the

23   reasonableness of Merck’s conduct in light of her experience and her understanding of FDA

24   regulations will be helpful to the jury.”).

 1          Instead, Ms. Cadman simply (1) identifies relevant statutes and regulations and

 2   (2) explains her conclusion that Defendants’ conduct violated those statutes and regulations. For

 3   example, Ms. Cadman notes that Thesis’s products contained Zembrin and that the FDA

 4   previously declined another company’s use of Zembrin in a dietary supplement because of

 5   “insufficient verification of safety for human consumption.” Dkt. 206-1 at 3. She then concludes

 6   that “[a]ccordingly, Zembrin’s inclusion in a dietary supplement—absent an accepted NDI

 7   notification or exemption—renders the product adulterated under 21 U.S.C. § 342(f)(1)(B).” Id.

 8   This “reads much like a legal memorandum, making legal conclusions, commenting on the

 9   applicable law, and applying the law to the facts.” Enberg v. Am. Home Assurance, No. CV-11-

10   125-BLG-RFC, 2013 WL 11821480, at *1 (D. Mont. Feb. 13, 2013). Aside from her reference to

11   a previous FDA decision—which she does not name or specify any details about—

12   Ms. Cadman’s expertise plays no role in her opinion that a product with Zembrin is “adulterated”

13   under federal law. Absent the use of any specialized knowledge, it is improper for Ms. Cadman

14   to advance these arguments “cloaked with the mantle of an expert” and perhaps “carry special

15   weight with the jury.” Jinro Am. Inc. v. Secure Invs., Inc., 266 F.3d 993, 1004 (9th Cir.), op.

16   amended on denial of reh’g, 272 F.3d 1289 (9th Cir. 2001).

17          Ms. Cadman’s opinion is not relevant under Daubert. The Court therefore GRANTS

18   Defendants’ motion to exclude Ms. Cadman. 2 Dkt. 206.
