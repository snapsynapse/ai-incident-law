On February 21, 2023, the named plaintiff, an African American man over the age of 40 who had a disability, filed this lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, individually and on behalf of a putative class. The plaintiff sued human and financial management software vendor Workday, Inc (“Workday”) under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Civil Rights Act of 1866 (42 U.S.C. § 1981), the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967, and the ADA Amendments Act of 2008 (“ADAAA”). Represented by private counsel, the plaintiff sought class certification, injunctive relief, declaratory judgment, monetary relief and damages. The plaintiff sought to represent a class of applicants or former applicants who were African American, over the age of 40, and/or had a disability. The plaintiff argued that Workday provided companies with algorithm-based applicant screening software that unlawfully determined whether or not an applicant should be accepted or rejected based on protected class characteristics of race, age, and disability. The case was assigned to Magistrate Judge Thomas S. Hixson but was later reassigned to Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. On July 17, 2023, the defendant filed a motion to dismiss. The defendant argued that it was a software provider that assisted customers with screening applicants, and not an employment agency. It also alleged that the plaintiff failed to exhaust intentional discrimination claims under Title VII and the ADEA and that neither Title VII nor the ADEA recognize a disparate impact claim against employment agencies. On September 13, 2023, the plaintiff filed a response in opposition to the defendant’s motion to dismiss arguing that Workday’s power to control access to employment opportunities made it an employment agency under Title VII, ADEA, and the ADA. On September 25, 2023, the defendant filed its reply to the plaintiff’s response. On November 27, 2023, the case was reassigned to District Judge Rita F. Lin. On January 19, 2024, the court granted the defendant’s request for dismissal with leave for the plaintiff to amend the complaint. The court found that the current complaint did not support the plaintiff’s argument that Workday was an employment agency, indirect employer, or agent, nor did it sufficiently allege specific employment practices causing a disparity sufficient to support a disparate impact claim. 2024 WL 208529. On February 20, 2024, the plaintiff filed an amended complaint alleging that the defendant acted as an employment agency, indirect employer, and agent, providing additional allegations relating to his job applications and rejections, and adding a claim for unlawful aiding and abetting of discrimination in violation of California’s Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA). On March 12, 2024, the defendant filed a motion to dismiss the plaintiff’s first amended complaint, arguing that it again failed to show Workday was an employment agency, indirect employer, or agent. On April 2, 2024, the plaintiff filed his opposition to the defendant’s motion to dismiss. He continued to allege that Workday was an entity subject to liability under Title VII, ADEA, and the ADA. On April 12, 2024, the defendant filed its reply, arguing that the plaintiff does not provide any plausible allegations suggesting that Workday “procures employees,” which is required to be an employment agency. On July 12, 2024, the court granted the defendant’s motion to dismiss in part. The motion was granted without leave to amend regarding the Title VII, ADEA, and ADA claims, to the extent they were based on an employment agency theory, and the intentional discrimination claims under Title VII, the ADEA, and Section 1981. The motion to dismiss the FEHA claim was granted with leave to amend. The court denied the motion to dismiss the disparate impact claims, reasoning that plaintiff had sufficiently alleged a specific employment practice – the use of algorithmic decision-making tools to screen applicants – resulting in a disparity – here, the plaintiff’s rejection from more than 100 jobs for which he was allegedly qualified, and that the specific employment practice caused that disparity. The court cited allegations that the plaintiff was “rejected at the screening stage from over 100 positions of a wide range and type, even though he was allegedly qualified for all of those jobs” as well as rejection emails received outside of business hours and within an hour after submitting applications, reasoning that “the sheer number of rejections and the timing of those decisions, coupled with the . . . allegations that Workday’s AI systems rely on biased training data . . . support a plausible inference that Workday’s screening algorithms were automatically rejecting [plaintiff’s] applications based on a factor other than his qualifications, such as a protected trait.” 740 F.Supp.3d 796. On August 2, 2024, the defendant filed an answer to the plaintiff’s amended complaint, denying the plaintiff’s allegations. On February 6, 2025, the plaintiff filed a motion for conditional certification of a collective action on his ADEA age discrimination claim, enforced pursuant to the Fair Labor Standards Act, which would allow him to notify similarly situated individuals of the lawsuit and provide them an opportunity to opt-in to having their claims heard on a collective basis. The court had a hearing on the motion on April 29, 2025. On May 16, 2025, the court granted Preliminary Collective Certification, finding that the plaintiff plausibly showed that all proposed collective members were subject to a common, unified policy that had an alleged disparate impact. 2025 WL 1424347. The court further held, on July 7, 2025, that the preliminary collective included applicants whose applications were scored, ranked, or screened using Workday’s HiredScore AI features. It rejected Workday’s arguments that HiredScore was acquired later, was a separate product, or used different algorithms, finding that those issues were better addressed at the decertification stage. Accordingly, Workday was required to identify customers using HiredScore AI features so that those applicants could be included in the collective. The court approved a plan for providing notice to prospective members of the collective on December 2, 2025. 2025 WL 3458887. "Whether to allow Amici to file a brief is solely within the Court's discretion, and generally courts have exercised great liberality[.] [T]here are no strict prerequisites that must be established prior to qualifying for amicus status; an individual seeking to appear as amicus must merely make a showing that his participation is useful or otherwise desirable to the court." Woodfin Suite Hotels, LLC v. City of Emeryville, No. C 06-1254 SBA, 2007 WL 81911, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 9, 2007) (cleaned up). As the EEOC is the federal agency tasked with enforcing the statutes under which many of Derek Mobley's claims arise, the EEOC's participation is useful to the Court on issues of first impression raised in Workday, Inc.'s 50 motion to dismiss. No. 63 ), and the Court will also consider Workday's responsive brief to the amicus brief. Accordingly, the EEOC's motion for leave to file an amicus brief is GRANTED, and the amicus brief (Dkt.
